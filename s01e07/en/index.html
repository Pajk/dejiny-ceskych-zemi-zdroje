1. Introduction

In the ninth century, a state stood in Moravia that dealt with Frankish kings, with the Pope in Rome and with the Emperor in Constantinople. It welcomed a mission that gave the Slavs their first alphabet of their own. And then, within a few years at the beginning of the tenth century, it mysteriously vanished. To this day we do not know where the fortress of its prince Rastislav stood, or where its archbishop Methodius is buried[19][6]. Today we call it Great Moravia.

Imagine a river island in southern Moravia, the eastern part of today's Czech Republic. Wooden ramparts all around; inside, stone churches with coloured plaster, the courts of nobles, goldsmiths at work. It is the ninth century, and the politics of Central Europe are being decided here.

We know about this state from two kinds of evidence. The first is chronicles – and most of them were written by its enemies. The second is archaeology, which has pulled thousands of graves and the foundations of churches out of the earth. Today we will look at both.

Before we start, a word about whom we can actually trust. Most of our information comes from the Annals of Fulda – a chronicle written in East Francia, which describes the Moravians through the eyes of their opponent[5].

A second view comes from a text from Salzburg known as the Conversio. Its author defended the Bavarian church's claim to the mission among the Slavs, and so he portrays Methodius as an intruder[2].

And the third voice belongs to the Old Church Slavonic lives of Constantine and Methodius, written by their disciples. They, on the contrary, admire their teachers[6]. A historian has to compare all three voices – and today we will do it with them.

We begin where the Moravians first appear in the sources: at an assembly in Frankfurt.

2. The Moravians Enter History

At the beginning of the ninth century, western Europe is ruled by the Frankish Empire, the legacy of Charlemagne. To the east lies the Byzantine Empire. And between them live Slavic tribes that have just freed themselves from the rule of the Avars. Charlemagne broke the Avar realm in wars at the end of the eighth century, and in the space that opened up, new Slavic states began to form.

In 822, Emperor Louis the Pious held an assembly in Frankfurt. The Frankish annals recorded who came bearing gifts: Obodrites, Sorbs, Wilzi, Bohemians – and Moravians, in Latin Marvani[1]. It is the first certain written mention of the Moravians.

About ten years later we have the name of the first known ruler: Mojmír. A Bavarian text on the spread of Christianity, the Conversio, says that Mojmír drove a prince named Pribina across the Danube[2]. The text gives no year; historians place the event at around 833[3]. According to a note added to the same text later, the archbishop of Salzburg, Adalram, consecrated a church on Pribina's estate at Nitrava – Nitra in today's Slovakia. That is why it is often said that Mojmír united Moravia with the Nitra region. It is a likely interpretation, but not a certain one.

Mojmír's rule then ended with an intervention from outside. Who would rule Moravia was decided by the East Frankish king.

3. Rastislav and the Indescribable Fortress

In August 846, the East Frankish king Louis the German marched into Moravia and placed Mojmír's nephew Rastislav on the throne[4]. What happened to Mojmír, we do not know – perhaps he was deposed, perhaps he was no longer alive.

The Franks hoped they had installed an obedient ally. But Rastislav soon began to play his own game. In 855, Louis marched against him again and failed: the annals speak of a very strong fortification that the Franks could not take[5]. And in 869 the chronicler writes of Rastislav's "indescribable fortress", unlike any older one[19].

Where did that fortress stand? It is one of the biggest questions in Czech archaeology. The candidates are Mikulčice and Staré Město near Uherské Hradiště – and we still do not know the answer[19].

But Rastislav did not defend himself with ramparts alone. His boldest step was not a military one.

4. An Embassy to Constantinople

Rastislav knew that Christianity was reaching Moravia mainly through Bavarian priests – and with them came Frankish influence. So he took a bold step. Around 862 he sent envoys to the Byzantine emperor Michael III and asked for a teacher who would explain the faith to the people in their own language[6].

The emperor chose two brothers from Thessaloniki: the scholar Constantine and his older brother Methodius, a former administrator of a Slavic province. Many Slavs lived around Thessaloniki, and the brothers knew their language well[7]. They arrived in Moravia in 863; some historians argue for 864[7].

For the mission, Constantine created a new script – Glagolitic – and with his brother he translated the liturgical books into the language we now call Old Church Slavonic[8]. Beware of a common mistake: Cyrillic, which bears Cyril's name, was created later in Bulgaria, probably among their disciples[26].

Worship in a Slavic language did not please the Bavarian clergy. According to Constantine's Life, his opponents claimed that God could be worshipped in only three languages – Hebrew, Greek and Latin. And so in 867 the brothers set off for Rome. Early in 868, Pope Hadrian II blessed their Slavonic books[8].

Constantine never returned to Moravia. In Rome he fell gravely ill, entered a monastery under the name Cyril, and died on the fourteenth of February 869. He is buried in the Basilica of Saint Clement[7]. At the end of 869 or early in 870, Methodius was consecrated archbishop with his seat at Sirmium, a see that was also meant to cover Moravia[8].

A different fate, however, awaited Rastislav, who had invited the brothers to Moravia.

5. Betrayal and the Rise of Svatopluk

Meanwhile, a drama was building in Moravia. Rastislav's nephew Svatopluk ruled a domain of his own – probably in the Nitra region. In 870 he fell out with his uncle and handed him over to the Franks. Rastislav was sentenced to death; King Louis had him "only" blinded and imprisoned. Where and when he died, we do not know[4].

At the same time, the Bavarians dealt with Methodius. A Bavarian synod condemned him, and according to his Life he spent some two and a half years in prison in Swabia. He was released only in 873, on the orders of Pope John VIII[6].

And Svatopluk? The Franks did not trust him and imprisoned him too. Meanwhile, the Moravians chose as their leader a priest named Slavomír, a member of the ruling family. The Franks then released Svatopluk and sent him with a Bavarian army against the rebels. But Svatopluk went over to the Moravians – and destroyed the Bavarian army[5]. He was both a traitor and a liberator, and historians still agree on that today.

In 874, Svatopluk made peace with the Franks at Forchheim. It was negotiated by his envoy, a priest named John of Venice. Svatopluk promised loyalty and a yearly payment, but in practice he won a free hand[5].

What he did with that free hand, the following years would show.

6. The Greatest Extent and Methodius's Archbishopric

Under Svatopluk, Moravia reached the height of its power. In 890, the East Frankish king Arnulf granted him rule over Bohemia[27]. According to a later legend, Bořivoj, the first historical member of the Přemyslid dynasty of Bohemia, was baptised by Methodius – but that is legend, not chronicle[9]. Methodius's Life mentions a powerful pagan prince on the Vistula; that Svatopluk conquered him is only a guess by historians[6][28]. That is why our map also shows dashed borders.

In June 880, Pope John VIII issued the letter Industriae tuae. In it he confirmed the Slavonic liturgy – the Gospel was to be read first in Latin – and a separate bishopric in Nitra for a Frank named Wiching[7][27]. An interesting detail: the Pope addresses Svatopluk with the Latin word comes – meaning ruler or prince, not king. He is called king by the Frankish chronicler Regino and, later, by one papal letter[27].

Wiching became Methodius's fiercest opponent. Methodius died on the sixth of April 885 and named his disciple Gorazd as his successor. Where he is buried, we do not know[6]. The very next year, at Wiching's urging and with Svatopluk's consent, his disciples were driven out of Moravia. Clement, Naum and others found refuge in Bulgaria – and it was there, in their circle, that Cyrillic probably came into being[8][26].

7. What the Archaeologists Found

The chronicles give us names and dates. Archaeology shows us how people lived here.

The most important site is Mikulčice-Valy near Hodonín. Its fortified core covered about 10 hectares, the whole settlement up to about 50 hectares. Since 1954, when Josef Poulík began the excavations, archaeologists have uncovered the foundations of twelve churches and more than 2,500 graves[14]. Among the churches is a three-aisled basilica – the largest known Great Moravian church[15]. The Institute of Archaeology in Brno has had a research base here since 1955, and the work continues to this day[14]. Since 1962, Mikulčice has been a Czech National Cultural Monument. Together with the church of Saint Margaret in nearby Kopčany, across the border in Slovakia, it is on the UNESCO Tentative List; the 2014 nomination, however, was withdrawn[15].

The church in Kopčany is often called the only Great Moravian building still standing. For a long time it was thought to be much younger. Only an excavation in 2004 found Great Moravian graves around it[30]. Its exact age, however, is still debated[22].

The second great centre lies at Staré Město near Uherské Hradiště. The cemetery Na Valách has about 1,500 graves and is the largest known Great Moravian burial ground. Many more graves had already been destroyed by sand quarrying in 1897[19]. At the end of the 1940s, the archaeologist Vilém Hrubý found the foundations of a church here – the first Great Moravian stone building discovered on Czech territory. Today the Great Moravia Memorial stands above them[19]. On the nearby hill of Sady stood an important church complex; that it was connected with Methodius is a tempting but unconfirmed hypothesis. Almost a thousand graves were found there, and the church at Sady is the only Great Moravian church known to have had a roof of fired tiles[20].

The third is Pohansko near Břeclav. Its fortified part covers 28 hectares, and Masaryk University has been excavating it since 1958. Here archaeologists found a nobleman's court, compared with Frankish royal palaces, and a church with more than 400 graves[17]. The court, about one hectare in size, was found in the very first season. The church had a single nave with a semicircular apse and a porch; it was built in the second half of the ninth century and disappeared at the beginning of the tenth. In the eleventh century, the role of a regional centre passed to nearby Břeclav[18].

What lies in the graves? Swords, pairs of spurs, gold and silver earrings, and above all gombíky – globular ornamental buttons that became a symbol of the Great Moravian elite. Some sword blades bear marks or inscriptions, and there is also silk, glass vessels and remains of glass windows[16]. These people were no "barbarians from the forest" – they were part of the European world.

And one more curiosity from the Břeclav area. In 2017, a piece of animal rib with seven Germanic runes was found in nearby Lány. But it is not Great Moravian – it dates from around 600, two hundred years earlier. It shows how carefully finds must be dated[23].

The world of the Great Moravian strongholds, however, did not last long.

8. The End

Svatopluk died in 894. The chronicler Regino of Prüm described him as a man "most prudent among his people and most cunning in mind"[13]. Half a century later, the Byzantine emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus told the story of the three rods: the dying Svatopluk supposedly showed his sons that one rod breaks easily, but three bound together do not. It is a moral tale, not a chronicle record.

a man most prudent among his people and most cunning in mind
Regino of Prüm, Chronicle, year 894

Reality matched it. As early as 895, the Bohemian princes Spytihněv and Vitislav paid homage to the Frankish king in Regensburg and left the Moravian sphere[9]. Svatopluk's sons, Mojmír II and Svatopluk II, fought each other in 898 and 899[13]. Mojmír II still tried to strengthen the state: around 900, papal legates consecrated an archbishop and three bishops for Moravia. The Bavarian bishops, led by Archbishop Theotmar of Salzburg, protested sharply in a letter to the Pope[10].

But a new power was coming from the east – the nomadic Magyars. The last mentions of the Moravians appear in the Raffelstetten customs regulation from the years 903 to 906, which refers to "the market of the Moravians"[12]. Around 906, Moravia ceased to exist as a power. And on the fourth of July 907, the Magyars destroyed a Bavarian army at a place called Brezalauspurc. It is the very first mention of today's Bratislava[11].

Bellum pessimum fecit ad Brezalauspurc IIII nonas Iulii
Annales Iuvavenses maximi, year 907

At Mikulčice, archaeology shows traces of violent events from the turn of the ninth and tenth centuries. Yet settlement continued there, only poorer[21]. Archaeologists have also found flood deposits from the same period, so historians today speak rather of several causes at once: the Magyar raids, an internal crisis, and perhaps nature as well[31]. It was not the people who vanished, but the state.

9. Legacy

By the way, the Moravians never called their state "Great Moravia". The name was first used around 950 by Constantine Porphyrogenitus, and historians still argue whether "great" means great, old, or distant[5].

Great Moravia disappeared, but its legacy did not. Methodius's disciples carried Slavonic writing to Bulgaria and from there to Rus. Bohemia, which had come into contact with Christianity under Svatopluk, took over the role of the region's centre in the tenth century – that is the subject of the next episode. And when the Moravian margrave Vladislaus Henry founded a Cistercian monastery near Staré Město in 1205, it was named Velehrad – after the old name of Staré Město, Veligrad. Its monks came from Plasy in Bohemia[19][32]. The brothers Cyril and Methodius are still honoured with a Czech state holiday on the fifth of July. The date itself, however, is not the anniversary of their arrival – the church chose it in the nineteenth century[25].

And where did Rastislav's indescribable fortress stand? Where is the grave of Saint Methodius? We do not know yet. Perhaps one of the next excavations will find out.
