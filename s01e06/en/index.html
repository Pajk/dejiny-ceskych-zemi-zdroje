1. An envoy before Samo

An envoy of the Frankish king Dagobert stands before a Slavic ruler. His name is Sicharius, and he has come to complain[1]. Slavs in this ruler's territory have allegedly killed Frankish merchants and taken their goods[1]. At first, the ruler refused to receive him. So Sicharius dressed in Slavic clothes and managed to gain an audience[1].

The negotiations went badly. The chronicler himself says the envoy behaved foolishly. He began making threats, claiming that the ruler and his people owed service to the king[1]. According to the chronicle, the ruler gave a conciliatory reply: the land and its people belonged to Dagobert, provided the king maintained their friendship[1]. Then the envoy declared that Christians, the servants of God, could not be friends with dogs[1]. The reply the chronicle puts into the ruler's mouth is sharp. If you are God's servants and we are God's dogs, then we may tear you with our teeth as long as you act against God[1]. The envoy was driven out[1].

If you are God's servants and we are God's dogs, then as long as you act against him, we are allowed to tear you with our teeth.
Chronicle of Fredegar, book IV, chapter 68

That ruler was Samo. According to the same chronicle, he was not a Slav but a merchant from the Frankish kingdom[1]. The chronicle places the scene in the ninth year of Dagobert's reign[1]. Its editor added the year 631, or perhaps the following year[1].

We know this whole story from a single account. It was recorded in a Frankish chronicle known as the Chronicle of Fredegar[19]. Its writer therefore stood on the other side of the dispute. Apart from a few references in Frankish sources, we have almost nothing about Samo[19].

Who were the people Samo ruled? When did they arrive, and where did they come from? In this episode, we will cover three centuries, from the 6th to the 8th century.

Throughout, we will distinguish three layers. The first is archaeology: what has been found in the ground. The second is written sources: what contemporaries wrote about the Slavs, usually foreigners and often enemies. The third is later legends. And we will begin with a legend.

2. A legend and the first traces

The legend is familiar to Czech audiences. Forefather Čech leads his people to Říp Mountain, and the land takes his name. The earliest written version is in the chronicle of Cosmas. In the Latin text, the elder is called Boemus, and his companions say the land should be named after him[6].

Since you, father, are called Boemus, let the land be called Boemia.
Cosmas, Chronicle of the Bohemians, book I, chapter 2

Cosmas wrote his chronicle between 1119 and 1125[22]. Roughly five hundred years separated him from the earliest Slavs. For the most ancient history, he drew on oral tradition, describing it himself as the fabulous tales of old men[23]. And he introduces the location near Říp with the words “as I believe”[6]. This is a legend written down in the 12th century, not an account of the Slavs' arrival. The forefather only received the name Čech in the Czech-language Chronicle of Dalimil, from the early 14th century[25].

What do archaeological finds tell us? Before the Slavs, Germanic peoples lived in the Czech lands, most recently the Lombards. In 568, they left the Danube region for northern Italy[11]. Archaeologists place the end of the Migration Period in the second half of the 6th century. Most of the Germanic inhabitants then moved towards the Danube, some continuing to northern Italy. And the first groups of Slavs gradually entered Bohemia, the western part of today's Czech Republic[11].

We recognise them by their pots. These were made by hand, without a potter's wheel, and were mostly undecorated[13]. In 1940, the archaeologist Ivan Borkovský described them and called them the Prague type[13]. Hence the name Prague-type pottery culture. It includes settlements of sunken huts and cremation graves, containing burned remains[18].

And when exactly did the Slavs arrive? Here, scholars disagree. Some place their arrival in Moravia no later than the middle of the 6th century. The historian Dušan Třeštík proposed the thirties of that century[13]. The archaeologist Dagmar Jelínková is more cautious. The earliest finds in Moravia that can be dated point to the turn of the 6th and 7th centuries[12]. But people must have arrived before their house was destroyed, or before an object we can now date was placed in a grave. Jelínková therefore suggests gradual settlement during the final third of the 6th century[12].

Why such a wide range? Radiocarbon dating of one house at Pavlov, in the Břeclav region, produced a range exceeding a hundred years[12]. Small metal objects that allow more precise dating are rare among the earliest Slavic finds[12].

We do not even know whether the newcomers encountered the last Germanic inhabitants. In Moravia, contact between the Slavs and the latest Lombard settlement has not yet been demonstrated archaeologically[12]. The end of Germanic settlement and the beginning of Slavic settlement therefore remain subjects of research[11].

We know more about how these people lived than about their arrival.

3. Roztoky: everyday life

Roztoky near Prague, on the left bank of the Vltava. Along a narrow strip beside the river, archaeologists have identified hundreds of sunken houses from the 6th to 7th centuries[7]. After the latest major excavation, more than 330 are known[8]. There may once have been as many as a thousand on the site[7]. The settlement stretches for more than a kilometre and a half[8].

Why here? We do not know. The archaeologists themselves describe the explanation as a mystery[7]. An unusually large community probably lived here, engaging not only in farming but perhaps also in crafts, trade or river transport[7].

What did a house look like? Timber has not survived in Roztoky's soil. What remains are postholes and, above all, stone ovens[9]. Finds include Prague-type pots, an antler comb and a pair of quern stones for grinding grain[7]. A railway and a road now cross the settlement[7].

Březno near Louny gives us an idea of these buildings. A major excavation there uncovered settlements from both the Migration Period and the Early Middle Ages[10]. An archaeological open-air museum has been taking shape on the site since 1981. Its houses follow ground plans uncovered by excavation and were built using old techniques and replica tools. Archaeologists even tried living in them[10]. But they remain modern reconstructions, not surviving ancient buildings.

And the graves? The earliest Slavs cremated their dead. The largest known cremation cemetery with Prague-type pottery is at Přítluky in southern Moravia[12]. Published counts vary from 436 to five hundred graves[12]. They contained few objects: glass beads, fragments of combs, spindle whorls, iron buckles and small knives[12].

That is archaeology. What did contemporaries write? The name of the Slavs first appears in written sources in the 6th century, in Constantinople[18]. The Byzantine historian Procopius writes that they are not ruled by one man, and that everyone decides on common affairs[4]. He says they live in poor huts, far apart, and often move[4]. They believe in one god, the maker of lightning, and sacrifice cattle to him[4]. Another author of the period, Jordanes, adds that instead of cities they have swamps and forests[3].

Instead of cities they have swamps and forests.
Jordanes, Getica, chapter 35

Both authors, however, describe Slavs far from the Czech lands. Jordanes places them near the Dniester and northwards as far as the Vistula[3]. The sources of this period first locate the Slavs north of the lower Danube[18]. And both writers view them from outside. In Central Europe, a written source attests to Slavs by the first half of the 7th century at the latest. That source is the Chronicle of Fredegar[17].

So where did they come from? Genetics is now trying to answer that question.

4. Where they came from: DNA evidence

The debate is old. Some scholars believe the Slavs migrated into Central Europe, most often proposing the territory of present-day Ukraine and Belarus as their starting point[17]. Others argue that mainly a language and way of life may have spread, while local people remained[17][18]. Pottery fragments cannot settle this. A pot cannot tell us what language its owner spoke[17].

DNA from bones could help. But the earliest Slavs cremated their dead, leaving a gap in samples from the most important period[17][18].

In 2025, two studies helped narrow that gap. The first analysed DNA from more than five hundred people, mainly from eastern Germany, Poland, northwestern Ukraine and the northwestern Balkans[18]. It found a large movement of people from Eastern Europe between the 6th and 8th centuries. In eastern Germany, Poland and Croatia, they replaced more than eighty percent of the earlier gene pool[18]. The authors also stress that the process varied between regions[18].

The second study concerns southern Moravia directly. It analysed the DNA of eighteen people from two sites near Břeclav, Líbivá and Pohansko[17]. The skeletons date from the 5th to the 10th centuries[17].

The result? People buried in the 5th century differ substantially in genetic terms from those buried later[17]. The change occurred by the beginning of the 8th century at the latest[17]. According to the authors, this is incompatible with the idea that descendants of the earlier inhabitants simply continued living here[17]. One of the earliest samples from the newer group comes from a newborn child at Pohansko. The child was buried without cremation, sometime in the 7th to 8th centuries[17]. From then until the 10th century, the study detects no major further change[17].

The authors themselves explain what their result does not say. Genes cannot tell us what language the newcomers spoke[17]. We cannot yet say how many arrived, or how quickly[17]. And we should add: eighteen people from two neighbouring cemeteries do not represent an entire country.

A powerful neighbour from the east now enters the story.

5. The Avars and the merchant Samo

In the sixties of the 6th century, the Avars arrived in the Carpathian Basin. They were nomads from the Central Asian steppes[19]. Their empire on the middle Danube lasted until around 800[18]. Many Slavs came under Avar rule[18].

The Chronicle of Fredegar describes what that rule was like. It calls the Avars Huns and the Slavs Wends[1]. According to its account, the Avars came to spend the winter among the Slavs every year, taking their wives and daughters and collecting tribute[1]. In battle, they made the Slavs fight while they themselves stood before the camp. When the Slavs won, the Avars went to take the spoils[1]. The chronicle mentions the word befulci, which it says the Avars used for the Slavs. Its exact meaning is uncertain. The chronicle's editor rejected the explanation offered by the chronicler himself[1].

This is a single source, written by an outsider. The archaeologist Jiří Košta points to another side of the relationship. The Slavs joined the Avars on campaigns against Byzantium, and their relationship may not always have disadvantaged them[19].

According to the chronicle, the sons of Avars and Slavic women eventually rebelled[1]. This is when Samo appears. The chronicle places his arrival in the fortieth year of King Chlothar's reign, corresponding to 623 to 624[1][19]. Two dates do not mean two events. The chronicle counts years from the king's accession, and these do not match our calendar years exactly[19]. It introduces Samo in a single sentence: a man named Samo, a Frank by birth from the district of Senonago[1].

A man named Samo, a Frank by birth from the district of Senonago.
Chronicle of Fredegar, book IV, chapter 48

Where that district lay is uncertain. The editor Bruno Krusch favoured the area around Sens in France, and Košta also leans towards this interpretation[1][19]. Other scholars argue for Soignies in present-day Belgium[1][24]. Even Samo's Frankish origin is disputed. A later Bavarian text describes him as a Slav[24].

Samo arrived with other merchants to trade[1]. The chronicle does not say what they traded[1][24]. He joined the Slavic army and proved himself so well that the Slavs elected him king[1]. He reportedly ruled for thirty-five years[1]. He had twelve Slavic wives[1]. With them, he had twenty-two sons, as well as fifteen daughters[1]. All these figures come from Fredegar, and we have no way to verify them independently[19].

Why elect a foreigner? Košta offers an explanation. Samo stood outside the ties between the individual Slavic groups, allowing them to unite under him[19]. This is a scholar's interpretation, not a statement in the source.

A few years later, Samo came into conflict with the Franks.

6. Wogastisburg

We return to the envoy Sicharius. After Samo drove him out, King Dagobert sent an army against the Slavs from Austrasia, one part of his kingdom[1].

According to the chronicle, it advanced in three columns[1]. The Lombards also marched against the Slavs, usually interpreted as acting in Dagobert's pay[1][24]. The Alemanni under Duke Chrodobert won, and so did the Lombards. Both took many captives[1]. But the Austrasians besieged a fortress the chronicle calls Wogastisburc. A strong Slavic force defended it[1]. They fought for three days. Then the Franks fled[1].

They fought for three days; many of Dagobert's men were killed by the sword, and the survivors fled home, leaving all their tents and belongings behind.
Chronicle of Fredegar, book IV, chapter 68

The chronicler offers his own explanation for the defeat. It was supposedly caused less by Slavic courage than by the disaffection of the Austrasians, who were at odds with Dagobert[1]. Here too, a Frank is writing mainly about Frankish politics.

The consequences were serious. The Slavs repeatedly raided Thuringia[1][2]. The Sorbian prince Dervan, previously subject to the Franks, joined Samo[1][19]. And the Thuringian duke Radulf later made friends with the Slavs[2].

And where did Wogastisburg stand? We do not know. The chronicle names no river, mountain or direction[1]. Everything else consists of scholars' proposals.

In the 19th century, the chronicle's editor added, with a question mark: in the Ohře valley[1]. Anton Gnirs looked near Vokov, not far from Cheb[21]. Ernst Schwarz connected it with Úhošť Hill near Kadaň, drawing on the similarity of the name[20]. For a long time, Úhošť was the most common answer[24]. The archaeologists Lutovský and Profantová proposed Rubín Hill near Podbořany, and Jiří Košta also considers it the most likely location[24][19]. Košta mentions the hillfort at Olomouc-Povel as another possibility[19]. Others look outside Bohemia, in Upper Franconia in present-day Bavaria. Heinrich Kunstmann and Hans Jakob proposed Burk near Forchheim[24].

Rubín shows how difficult the question is. Košta points to finds from the 7th century in its favour[19]. But Naďa Profantová and Daniel Stolz later reviewed what is actually known from that period. There are two fittings of western origin and one Avar-type strap end, itself uncertain[14]. We do not know when the hillfort was fortified[14]. And one interpretation suggests Wogastisburg need not have been a hillfort at all, but perhaps only a lightly fortified camp[20].

Samo's empire is just as uncertain.

7. Samo's “empire”

Fredegar describes Samo as king of the Slavs and speaks of his kingdom[1]. Samo's empire is a label used by modern historians[19]. Rather than a state, it was probably a union of tribes held together by common defence[19].

How large was it, and where was its centre? Scholars disagree[24]. Jiří Košta places its core in Moravia, part of Lower Austria and southwestern Slovakia. In his interpretation, the rebellion against the Avars began there[19]. He also includes Bohemia in the union and suggests Samo's influence may have reached Carinthia[19]. The chronicle mentions another Slavic duke there, Valluk. But it does not say he was subject to Samo[2].

Other scholars look for the centre further northwest, in Bohemia and Upper Franconia[24]. The historian Martin Eggers summarised these views in a survey article. He himself even excludes Moravia and Carinthia from Samo's territory[24]. Even Košta cannot determine the northern and eastern limits[19]. The chronicle itself gives no borders[1]. That is why Samo's union appears on our map with a dashed outline, representing only one interpretation.

Why did the union emerge at that particular moment? Košta notes that the Avar empire was in crisis. In 626, the Avars unsuccessfully besieged Constantinople, and the defeat weakened their power[19].

According to Fredegar, Samo ruled for thirty-five years[1]. Adding this to the date of his election places his death shortly before 660[19]. This is only a calculation based on Fredegar's figures. The chronicle says nothing about Samo's death, his seat of power or his burial place[1].

The union disappears from the sources along with Samo and probably did not long outlive him[19]. Košta explains this by suggesting that the reason for its existence had faded. The external threat had weakened[19].

After Samo, a long silence falls over the written sources for the Czech lands.

8. The eighth century: hillforts and Charlemagne

Historians describe this as a period poor in written sources[20]. Archaeological finds do most of the talking.

In the 8th century, we see hillforts, fortified seats of the elite[15][16]. Mikulčice in southern Moravia was already an important centre of power[16]. Hooked spurs and bronze ornaments of Avar origin provide evidence[16]. But graves from that period have not yet been found there[16].

What about Bohemia? Similar places are known there too. Older collections from Rubín Hill near Podbořany contain the largest number of Avar-origin fittings found in Bohemia[14]. At Tismice east of Prague, a hillfort covers more than 20 hectares[15]. About 170 non-ferrous metal ornaments have been found there, mainly from belts and horse harnesses[15]. Some were made on the site[15]. Radiocarbon dates indicate occupation from the final third of the 8th century[15].

Archaeologists classify such ornaments as objects displaying power and status[15]. But a fitting alone cannot tell us who wore it.

At the end of the 8th century, the Avar empire declined[16]. From the other direction, Charlemagne's Frankish empire turned towards the Czech lands. In 805, Charlemagne sent his son with an army into the land of the Slavs whom the Frankish annals call Beheimi[5]. The army ravaged the country and killed their duke, named Lecho[5]. Another Frankish chronicle speaks of three armies meeting on the Ohře and besieging a fortress called Canburg[20]. We do not know where it stood either[20].

A year later, another Frankish army came, from Bavaria, Alemannia and Burgundy[5]. According to the annals, it devastated a considerable part of the country and returned without serious losses[5].

And in 822, envoys of the Bohemians and Moravians stood before the emperor at Frankfurt, bringing gifts[5]. That is where our next episode begins.

Let us review what we know about these three centuries.

9. What to take away

First: the culture of the earliest Slavs appears in the Czech lands in the 6th century, probably in its second half. We do not know an exact date, because the evidence rests on pottery fragments and a few dated finds[11][12][13].

Second: these people lived in sunken houses with stone ovens and cremated their dead. At Roztoky near Prague, archaeologists have identified more than 330 such houses[7][8][9].

Third: DNA from southern Moravia shows a substantial population change between the 5th and 8th centuries. Genes cannot tell us what language the newcomers spoke[17].

Fourth: almost everything we know about Samo comes from a single Frankish chronicle. According to it, he became king of the Slavs in 623 to 624. He reportedly ruled for thirty-five years[1].

Fifth: around 631, the Slavs defeated the Franks at Wogastisburg. We do not know where that was[1][19].

And the legend of Forefather Čech was only written down by Cosmas in the 12th century[6][22].

Two questions remain open. Where was Wogastisburg? And where was the core of Samo's union: in Moravia and along the Danube, or in Bohemia and further west? Only a fortunate discovery may answer the first. Perhaps not even that can answer the second.

Next time: Great Moravia.
