1. The Head from the Sand Pit

In May 1943, Josef Šlajchrt was tidying up his sand pit near Mšecké Žehrovice, a village west of Prague. Thieves had been taking sand from it on the sly, and the ground needed levelling[7][8]. In the earth he found several pieces of a stone head[7].

He did not keep them. He reported the find to the keeper of the museum in the nearby town of Nové Strašecí, who informed the State Archaeological Institute in Prague. That same year the archaeologist Ivan Borkovský dug at the spot[7].

The head is carved from marlstone, a local rock[1][7]. Today it is put together from four parts. The fragment with the left ear is missing and was never found[7]. The man has a moustache, a neck ring around his neck, and the back of his head is shaved[1][7]. Archaeologists date the sculpture to the 3rd century BC[1][7]. It is one of the best-known La Tène finds from Bohemia and belongs to the collections of the National Museum in Prague[1][7].

Whom does the face show? A god, a hero, or a priest? We will come to that. In this episode we will cover roughly four and a half centuries: from warrior graves, through the first coins, to the large fortified settlements we call oppida. And we will end with the name Bohemia.

One thing is new compared with the previous episodes. Now, for the first time, ancient authors also write about Central Europe[1]. So we will carefully keep apart what the finds say and what the texts say. The two do not always agree.

We begin with who the Celts actually were.

2. The Celts and the La Tène Culture

We know the name Celts from the Greeks. The first report of them was written down by the historian Herodotus in the 5th century BC: according to him, the river Danube rises in the land of the Celts[1]. Ancient authors also called these people Gauls[1]. They meant the barbarians who lived north and west of the Alps[1].

Archaeologists have a term of their own: the La Tène culture. It is named after the site of La Tène on Lake Neuchâtel in Switzerland[1]. We recognise it mainly by a shared style of decoration on jewellery, weapons and vessels[1]. Finds of this culture reach from Ireland to western Ukraine[1].

And now an important note. Celts is a term of historians and linguists. The La Tène culture is a term of archaeologists. History and linguistics place the Celts roughly where archaeology finds the La Tène culture[1]. But neither a sword nor a brooch will tell us what language its owner spoke, or which tribe he counted himself part of[1]. So when I say Celts in this episode, I mean the people of the La Tène culture, who were given that name by their southern neighbours.

Bohemia belonged to La Tène Europe. The La Tène culture gradually covered almost all of its territory. The exception was the north of Bohemia, where the northern edge of the whole La Tène culture ran[1].

The new style appeared here as early as the 5th century BC[16]. At that time the Hallstatt period, with which the last episode ended, was still fading out. Archaeologists therefore count this earliest La Tène stage as part of its close[1][16]. To it belong the richest male graves of the time, which are called princely. They contain two-wheeled chariots, swords and bronze vessels imported from the south[16]. And the hillfort of Závist above the Vltava was then one of the most important centres in Bohemia[3]. We will come back to it.

How did the culture get here? The Roman historian Livy tells a story about it. According to him, the Celts in Gaul were ruled by King Ambigatus, and the land was overpopulated. So he sent two nephews to look for new homes. The lot gave Segovesus the Hercynian Forest; to Bellovesus the gods showed the way to Italy[12]. By the Hercynian Forest, ancient authors meant the woodlands said to stretch from the sources of the Danube to the Carpathians[1].

It is a legend. Livy wrote around the turn of the era, and he sets the story in the time of the Roman king Tarquinius Priscus, that is, whole centuries earlier[1][12]. Specialists describe his account as a myth[1].

What the finds themselves say about those centuries is shown mainly by graves.

3. Warrior Graves

At the beginning of the 4th century BC, the burial custom in Bohemia changed within a short time. Until then people had mostly cremated their dead. Now they began to lay them in the ground unburnt, in graves without mounds[1]. Archaeologists call these burial grounds flat inhumation cemeteries. They lie mainly in the northern half of Bohemia[1].

The largest one we know comes from Jenišův Újezd, in north-western Bohemia. At the end of the 19th century, at least 120 graves were dug up there[1]. It is still the largest cemetery of its kind in Central Europe, and burials went on there throughout the 4th and 3rd centuries[1].

What lies in the graves? Some of the men were given weapons: an iron sword, and usually a spear and a shield as well[1]. Women with rich grave goods have bronze anklets, bracelets, brooches and sometimes a neck ring. Later, bracelets of black sapropelite and of glass become more common[1].

To the same period belongs a famous find from 1882. In the thermal Giant Spring at Lahošť near Duchcov lay a bronze cauldron, and in it at least two thousand bronze objects, mainly brooches and ring jewellery[1]. One type of brooch took its name from this find, and the name is still in use: the Duchcov brooch[1].

Do the new graves mean that new people arrived? The older interpretation said yes. It relied on ancient reports and saw in the cemeteries the trace of large tribes that had occupied a new land[1]. Since the 1960s the opposite view has gained ground: that the local population went on developing here[1]. Today the change is explained mainly as a change of elites, accompanied by a transformation of society and belief[1]. According to the current assessment of the finds, it was rather only small groups that moved, military or otherwise[1].

Scientific analyses that can tell which of the buried grew up elsewhere could decide the matter[1]. For now, then, it remains open.

But the ancient texts do name one tribe from Central Europe. They are the Boii.

4. The Boii between Italy and the Hercynian Forest

The Boii enter the written sources at the beginning of the 4th century BC, when Celts occupied northern Italy[1]. According to ancient authors they came from somewhere in the north, from beyond the Alps[1][12]. The Greek historian Polybius lists them among the tribes settled beyond the river Po, below the Apennines[13].

After that the Boii fought Rome again and again[13]. In 193 BC the Romans defeated them near the town of Mutina[1][12]. Soon afterwards the Boii surrendered to Rome and lost almost half of their land[12]. The geographer Strabo later wrote that the Boii were driven out of Italy and went to the regions around the Danube[11].

So did they return to Bohemia? Here we must be careful. Ancient reports speak of the Boii many times, but never explicitly in connection with the territory of Bohemia[1]. Nor do they say whether, after the defeat in Italy, they came here too[1].

The closest thing is a report that Strabo took over from the earlier scholar Posidonius. The Boii are said to have formerly lived in the Hercynian Forest. The Germanic Cimbri attacked them there, but the Boii drove them back[11]. Researchers place that clash between 120 and 113 BC[1].

The Boii formerly lived in the Hercynian Forest
Strabo, Geography 7, 2, 2 (after Posidonius)

But the Hercynian Forest is not the same thing as Bohemia. Where exactly the Boii lived is still debated today. It seems beyond doubt that it was Central Europe north of the Danube. It is not clear, however, whether all of Bohemia belonged to them, only its south or west, or neighbouring Bavaria as well[1]. And the Boii need not have been alone here. The Roman general Caesar places yet another Celtic tribe by the Hercynian Forest[1].

And archaeology? Graves in Bohemia have yielded jewellery that comes from northern Italy, for example neck rings of twisted wire[1]. It shows that the two regions were in contact. The name of a tribe, however, is not written on it.

The Boii in Bohemia are therefore an assumption that specialists routinely work with. It is not a certainty[1].

Let us now return to the sand pit and its stone head. Years later, the archaeologists went back there too.

5. The Residence at Mšecké Žehrovice

Between 1979 and 1988, archaeologists investigated the find spot again[1]. It turned out that the head had not lain in a lonely pit. Right beside it there had stood a whole complex, and it had two periods.

First there was an unenclosed settlement of craftsmen. They smelted iron and above all worked sapropelite[1]. Sapropelite is a black rock that comes to the surface in this part of central Bohemia. It was made mainly into rings and bracelets[1].

Later, a rectangular enclosure grew up on the same spot, the residence of someone important. At first it was protected by a timber fence, then by a bank with a ditch. The banked complex consisted of two square parts, each about one hectare in area. In one of them stood a large house of unusual construction[1].

When exactly? Here we come up against the limit of one method. Radiocarbon dating is imprecise for the years 800 to 200 BC and gives only wide ranges[1]. Archaeologists therefore date mainly by objects. They place the building and rebuilding of the large house around 170 to 140 BC. The later of the two dates is uncertain[1].

Similar rectangular earthworks were long called sanctuaries. The excavation at Mšecké Žehrovice changed that interpretation. Today such complexes are explained as residences, the seats of a local elite, where people lived, farmed, gathered and most likely held rituals as well[1].

And the head? Its fragments lay in a pit outside the enclosure[1]. Originally researchers took it for an image of a deity[1]. A more recent interpretation starts from the hairstyle. The shaved back of the head and the band of hair may mark a druid, a member of the priestly class that ancient authors write about[1][7]. On this reading the sculpture shows a respected ancestor whom the local kin group honoured as a hero[1][7].

That is an interpretation, not a label. The sculpture has no inscription. All that is certain is that it survived broken and that its fragments lay in a pit[1][7].

While the residence stood at Mšecké Žehrovice, something much larger was growing on the hills above the Vltava and the Berounka.

6. The Oppida

We call them oppida. Caesar used the word for fortified settlements in Gaul, and archaeologists adopted it[1]. An oppidum is a large, strongly fortified settlement on high ground, usually covering tens of hectares[1].

In Bohemia we now know six of them: Závist, Stradonice, Hrazany, Nevězice, Třísov and České Lhotice[1][15]. All lie in the hill country of the central, southern and eastern parts of the land, high above river valleys[1]. In Moravia, Staré Hradisko near Prostějov belongs with them[1][5]. The Bohemian oppida arose around the middle of the 2nd century BC[1][3].

Let us start with Závist, on a hill above the Vltava south of Prague[3]. On the site of an older hillfort, what is probably the oldest oppidum north of the Alps came into being here[1][3]. The fortified area grew step by step until it reached 118 hectares[1]. The Archaeological Atlas counts almost two hundred hectares with all the outer baileys[3].

We should not picture the rampart as a bank of earth. Its face was a dry-stone wall strengthened with upright timbers, and behind it was a body of earth and wood[1]. It used to be three to three and a half metres high[1]. But timber rotted. One rampart could last 25 to 30 years and then had to be built again[1]. At Závist archaeologists have distinguished five building phases of the fortification[1].

Was it only for defence? Most likely not. According to current views, ramparts and gates also displayed the standing and prestige of the place[1].

The second great oppidum, Stradonice above the Berounka, covered about 90 hectares[1][4]. Its fate is a sad one. After gold coins were found there, treasure hunters dug it over at the end of the 19th century. They sold the metal objects to collectors, and the bones from the dug-up layers to a sugar factory[4]. Even so, thousands of objects come from the site, along with one of the largest collections of La Tène glass in Europe[1]. Among them are things from Italy: medical instruments and writing equipment as well[1].

How many people lived here? Estimates tend to be exaggerated. The archaeologists who excavated Závist judge that at Závist and Stradonice one can imagine something like a thousand inhabitants. At little Nevězice, hardly one to two hundred[1]. These are estimates based on the density of buildings, not a census[1].

What is certain is that the oppida were places of production: iron was forged, bronze was cast, glass and amber were worked, and coins were struck[1]. Whether someone also ruled the whole region from there is something researchers do not agree on[1].

In Moravia things developed a little differently. At Němčice nad Hanou, a large unfortified centre existed even before the oppida arose[1][6]. Over two thousand coins were found there and, according to the excavators, the oldest glass workshop north of the Alps documented so far[6]. A similar centre lay on the same trade route at Roseldorf in Lower Austria[6]. Staré Hradisko then controlled the long-distance trade between the Baltic and northern Italy. Baltic amber was worked there[5].

Amber, glass, and wine from the south[1][5]. Where there is trade, there tends to be money too.

7. Golden Rainbow Cups

For centuries people called them rainbow cups. The Czech name is a loose translation of a German word that is attested as early as the beginning of the 17th century[1]. People believed that such coins could be found where a rainbow had touched the ground[17]. Finds of these coins drew attention long before archaeology existed[1].

The first coins began to be struck in our lands around the middle of the 3rd century BC. But the exact dates here are only a rough estimate[1]. It was not a home-grown idea. The oldest pieces imitate the gold staters of Alexander the Great: on the front the head of the goddess Athena in a helmet, on the back a standing Nike, the goddess of victory[1]. Celtic mercenaries most likely came across such coins in the Greek lands[1]. A stater weighed about eight and a half grams of gold[1].

Over time the image changed, until all that was left was a bump on one side and rays with a crescent on the other. These coins are called shell staters. What the image means, we do not know. It is almost certainly not a shell[1]. These are the coins that were struck in the time of the oppida[1].

Specialists speak of Boian coins. But that is a name for a whole system of coinage rather than a label with the name of a tribe. The same coins are also found in large numbers in Austria north of the Danube and in south-western Slovakia[1].

The largest hoard came to light in 1771 near Podmokly, in western Bohemia. In a bronze cauldron lay an estimated five to ten thousand gold coins[1]. They weighed thirty to fifty kilograms[1]. More than 99% of the find ended up in the melting furnace[1]. The cauldron also held a twisted gold ring. That is why it has been suggested that this was an offering to the gods. It could just as well have been the treasure of a tribe or of its chief[1].

The hoard from Stradonice, found in 1877, met a similar end. About seven hundred coins had originally lain in a leather pouch, and almost all of them were melted down[1]. The largest hoard that has survived comes from Starý Kolín, east of Prague: over three hundred gold coins[1].

The coins also reveal long-distance contacts. At Stradonice, money from Bavaria and from Gaul was found, and also bronze coins of the Numidian king Massinissa and his successors[1].

Striking coins was not a matter for an individual. Someone had to organise and control it, for example at the level of a whole tribe[1].

And yet that world came to an end within a few decades.

8. The End of the Oppida and the Name Bohemia

One thing about the people of the oppida we do not know at all: where their dead are. Burial in the flat cemeteries had ended even before the oppida arose. From the time of the oppida we do not know a single certain La Tène grave from Bohemia[1]. Perhaps the bodies were treated in a way that left no trace in the ground[1].

And then the oppida end too. Exactly when is disputed. The Archaeological Atlas places their end around the middle of the 1st century BC[3][4]. The excavators of Závist and Stradonice date it, on the evidence of the finds, as late as around 30 BC[1]. So a range is safer: between the middle of the century and its last decades.

What happened? We have no single explanation. Objects of foreign cultures from the north and north-west appear among the finds of that time[1]. La Tène settlement apparently weakened[1]. But La Tène pottery and stone hand mills are still found in the settlements of the next culture, one spread mainly in central Germany[1]. The end of the La Tène culture is therefore usually linked with the arrival of the first Germanic groups[1]. Some researchers see in it a replacement of the population, others a gradual merging of what was left of the old population with the newcomers[1].

Meanwhile the written reports follow the Boii elsewhere. In 58 BC, some of the Boii march into Gaul with the Helvetii, as Caesar writes[14][1]. Then, some time around the middle of the century, the Boii in Pannonia, on the middle Danube, were defeated by the Dacians[1][11]. Strabo, who writes about it, gives no year. He only names the Dacian king Burebista[11]. The Czech summary work places the defeat in 41 or 40 BC[1]. Whether these were Boii from Bohemia, the sources do not say[1].

But the name remained. At the beginning of our era, Strabo writes of a place called Buiaimon in the Hercynian Forest. Living there by then is Maroboduus, king of the Germanic Marcomanni[11][2]. The Roman officer Velleius Paterculus knows the same land as Boiohaemum[10]. And in AD 98 the historian Tacitus wrote: “The name Boihaemum still survives and recalls the old history of the place, although the inhabitants have changed.”[9][2]

The name Boihaemum still survives and recalls the old history of the place, although the inhabitants have changed.
Tacitus, Germania 28

Boiohaemum means the land of the Boii[1]. From this name came the word Bohemia[11].

Notice what these three reports say and what they do not. All of them come from a time when Germanic rulers already held the land. They show that the region bore a name after the Boii. They do not give its exact borders. It is researchers who link it with Bohemia[1][2].

Perhaps something else remained after the Celts. The ancient names of some Bohemian mountain ranges are held to be Celtic. For rivers such as the Elbe or the Ohře, today's linguistics prefers an origin that is older still[1].

Let us sum up what to remember from the La Tène period.

9. What to Remember

First: Celts is a name from ancient texts, the La Tène culture is a term of archaeologists. The two roughly overlap, but the objects themselves reveal neither language nor tribe[1].

Second: from the beginning of the 4th century BC we know flat cemeteries in Bohemia, with the graves of warriors and of richly furnished women. Whether they were founded by newcomers or by local people remains open[1].

Third: the Boii are named only by ancient authors. That they lived in Bohemia is an assumption. Where exactly they lived is not known[1][11].

Fourth: around the middle of the 2nd century BC the oppida arose, for example Závist and Stradonice. They were places of production and trade, and gold coins were struck there[1][3][4].

And fifth: the oppida came to an end in the second half of the 1st century BC. But the name Boiohaemum, the land of the Boii, remained, and it lives on in the word Bohemia[1][9][11].

Two questions remain open. Whom does the head from Mšecké Žehrovice show? And what became of the people of the oppida: did they leave, or did they merge with the newcomers? The first question we may never settle. New research may help with the second.

Next time come the Germanic tribes, and with them, for the first time, Roman legions.
