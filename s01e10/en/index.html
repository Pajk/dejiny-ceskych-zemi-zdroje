1. A Golden Bull that is not golden

In an underground vault of the National Archives in Prague lies a parchment charter with a seal hanging from it. We call it the Golden Bull of Sicily. But the seal is not made of solid gold. It is hollow.[9][15]

And it is not even a single charter. There are three charters with three seals. They were issued on the same day, 26 September 1212, in Basel, Switzerland, by the young Frederick II. At the time, he had only been elected Roman emperor, and he was also King of Sicily. And he sealed them with the seal of his Sicilian chancery. Hence the name.[3][9][15]

The main charter went to the Bohemian king Přemysl Ottokar I. It is one of the most famous documents in Czech history. The National Archives sums it up like this: it confirms the hereditary royal title, the election of the king at home in Bohemia, and the borders of the Czech state.[3][27]

How did Přemysl get it? Mainly through skilful politics. For years, he switched between the rivals who were fighting over rule in the Holy Roman Empire.[1][4][8]

Today's episode covers the half-century in which Bohemia became a lasting kingdom. We begin with a crown in 1198. Then comes a divorce that even the pope got involved in, and the bull itself. We will see how the king fell out with a bishop, and how Princess Agnes refused an emperor. Then come new towns and silver, fear of the Mongols, and finally a war between father and son. We end in 1253.[1][3][6]

2. A crown from Philip

At the end of the last episode, the brothers Přemysl and Vladislav Henry came to an agreement. Přemysl ruled in Bohemia, Vladislav in Moravia. And Přemysl immediately saw an opportunity.[2]

In the Empire, two men were fighting over the royal throne at the time: Philip of Swabia and Otto IV. Each of them needed allies. Přemysl sided with Philip and marched with him to the Rhine.[2][4][8]

But on the way came disgrace. Abbot Jarloch of Milevsko recorded that the Bohemian army mutinied near Würzburg. The armed men abandoned their lords and went back home to Bohemia. Only the nobles stayed with the duke.[2]

Even so, it worked out. On 8 September 1198, Philip was consecrated king in Mainz. And Jarloch adds that there Philip also made our duke king of Bohemia.[2]

Our duke, consecrated, he makes king of Bohemia.
Jarloch of Milevsko, under the year 1198, our translation from the Latin

With this report, Jarloch's chronicle ends. As if the chronicler felt it was a good ending.[2]

It was already the third Bohemian crown. Before Přemysl, only two Bohemian rulers had gained the royal title: Vratislav II and Vladislav II. But their sons were no longer kings. Přemysl wanted the crown to stay in the family.[28]

3. A divorce and the pope

But the new king had a problem at home. He had been married for about twenty years to Adela of Meissen. They had a son, Vratislav, and three daughters. Now he wanted to be rid of her.[7]

Probably early in 1199, the Bishop of Prague declared their marriage invalid. The reason was kinship in the fourth degree. Adela was not even allowed to attend the hearing. She appealed to Pope Innocent III.[7]

Meanwhile, Přemysl married again. The new queen was Constance, sister of the King of Hungary.[1][7]

And then Přemysl showed how well he could switch sides. In 1203, he left Philip and went over to Otto IV. At the time, Otto had the pope's support.[1][8]

Pope Innocent wrote him a letter for this, and it has survived. Philip's crown, he says, is not valid. Philip himself had not been properly crowned, and so he could not properly crown Přemysl either.[4]

Since he himself had not been lawfully crowned, he could not lawfully crown either you or anyone else.
Innocent III to Přemysl, 19 April 1204, our translation from the Latin

That letter says one more thing. Before Přemysl, many had worn a royal diadem in Bohemia. But the popes had never called them kings in their letters. Přemysl is the first they will call king.[4]

It did not last long. As early as 1205, Přemysl returned to Philip. According to the annals, he had to hand over hostages and seven thousand marks for it. And in 1207, he betrothed his son Wenceslas to Philip's daughter Kunigunde.[1]

The dispute with Adela dragged on for more than ten years. It ended only with her death, in a convent near Meissen, in 1211.[7]

4. Three charters from Basel

A few years later, Otto ruled the Empire as emperor. But even that did not last. In 1211, Přemysl turned against Otto. And a year later, a new contender arrived in Germany: Frederick, King of Sicily.[1][8]

Přemysl was among the first to join him. And Frederick says so explicitly in the bull. Ottokar, he writes, was the first among the princes to elect him emperor.[3]

First among the princes, and especially before all the others, he elected us emperor.
Golden Bull of Sicily, 1212, our translation from the Latin

What does the charter say? Frederick confirms Ottokar's royal title, just as Philip had granted it. He gives him the Kingdom of Bohemia freely and without any payment. To him and to his successors, forever.[3]

Whoever is elected king in Bohemia only has to come to the emperor to receive the regalia. The charter does not explicitly proclaim a hereditary monarchy. But it does speak of the king's successors and heirs. And that is exactly what historians read in it: the crown belongs to the dynasty, and the Bohemians elect their king themselves.[3][4][27]

The king may install the bishops of his land in office. He holds all the borders that belong to the kingdom, however they may have been taken from it. And his duties to the Empire are small. He only has to attend imperial assemblies in Bamberg and Nuremberg, and exceptionally in Merseburg. And when the emperor travels to Rome to be crowned, the king sends three hundred armoured men, or pays three hundred marks.[3][4][27]

The other two charters concern property. Přemysl received estates in Franconia and in the Vogtland. His brother, the Moravian margrave Vladislav Henry, received a territory called Mocran et Mocran. Historians argue about what that means. According to one interpretation, it is simply Moravia; according to others, nobody knows to this day.[4][9][27]

Among the witnesses of the bull, we also find Count Rudolf of Habsburg. That name will come back in our story.[3]

And what about the gold? According to the archivists, the seal is hollow, made of thin gilded metal. A really solid piece of gold only hangs from the charter of Charles IV. He confirmed the bull a hundred and thirty-six years later, in 1348.[9][15]

The bull also had an adventurous fate. In 1750, it was taken to Vienna together with most of the crown archive. It only came back in 1920. And the name Golden Bull of Sicily, as we know it today, only became established in the First Republic, interwar Czechoslovakia. All three charters were exhibited together for the first time only in 2018.[9][15]

5. King against bishop

Přemysl immediately made use of what he had gained. In 1216, he had his son Wenceslas elected king. Frederick confirmed the election in Ulm with a charter. Anyone who violated it was to pay a thousand pounds of gold. In it, Wenceslas is called the firstborn son, even though Vratislav, the son of the cast-off Adela, was still alive.[4]

But Ondřej, the Bishop of Prague, stood up against the king. The question was who would decide about church property and about priests.[1][4][8]

The bishop reached for the strongest weapon he had. On 10 April 1216, he placed Bohemia under an interdict. Throughout the land, no Mass could be celebrated, and no church funerals could be held. And he himself left for Rome.[1]

The interdict was lifted a year later by the Archbishop of Mainz. But in 1219, Ondřej declared it again.[1]

In the end, the dispute was settled by a papal legate. The pope ordered him to find a safe place for the bishop to negotiate, because with the king and his lords it would be dangerous for him. A record survives of the negotiations in 1221. In it, the bishop demands his estates back, together with their charters. And he protests that it is not enough to return the parchment and ink to him.[4]

It was not enough for him to have the parchment and ink returned.
record of the negotiations, 2 July 1221, our translation from the Latin

The legate made both sides give way. In 1222, Přemysl issued a privilege for churches and monasteries. One of its sentences shows how things used to be. It abolishes a custom under which priests at the border toll were treated as worse than Jews.[4][8]

But Bishop Ondřej never returned to Bohemia. He died in exile in Rome in 1224.[1]

In 1228, Wenceslas and his wife Kunigunde were crowned in Prague. And on 15 December 1230, Přemysl Ottokar I died. For the first time in Czech history, a son who already had a crown came to the throne.[1]

6. The princess who refused an emperor

The daughter of Přemysl and Constance was born in 1211. Her name was Agnes. When she was just three, she was sent to Silesia, now part of Poland, to be brought up by her aunt, Saint Hedwig. Then she lived with the Premonstratensian nuns at Doksany, and from the age of eight at the court of the Duke of Austria.[6]

At that time, a princess was above all a political pledge. She was betrothed to the son of a Silesian duke, then to Henry, son of Emperor Frederick.[6]

How such brides were negotiated is shown by a letter from an English bishop in 1225. He wrote to his king that France, Hungary, England and Bohemia were all competing for the hand of young Henry.[4]

The Bohemian king reportedly offered thirty thousand marks. The Duke of Bavaria arrived with great pomp and added another fifteen thousand. But Henry reportedly told him that he would never marry Agnes. And about Emperor Frederick, the bishop passed on the words of the Archbishop of Cologne: he thirsts for nothing but money.[4]

He thirsts for nothing but money.
Bishop of Carlisle to the English king, 1225, our translation from the Latin

Nothing came of the marriage. Then the emperor himself wanted Agnes. This is attested by a letter written to her by Saint Clare of Assisi. In it, she writes that Agnes could have been married, with all glory, to the illustrious emperor, but she refused it all.[5][6]

Agnes chose a different path. In the early 1230s, she had a convent built in Prague, on the bank of the Vltava, for the sisters of Saint Clare, together with a hospital for the poor and the sick. The convent first appears in charters in 1234. And in the same year, she entered it herself.[6][11]

The Convent of St Agnes is one of the oldest Gothic buildings in Bohemia. Its builders soon switched from small stone blocks to fired bricks.[17][19]

The hospital brotherhood became an order in 1237. Its brothers later wore a red star. To this day, we call them the Knights of the Cross with the Red Star. And in 1252, a new hospital was built for them at the foot of the Prague bridge.[1][6]

But Agnes was not abbess for long. In 1238, she gave up that office and lived on as an elder sister.[6]

Her mother Constance also founded a convent. Near Tišnov in Moravia, the Cistercian convent of Porta coeli, the Gate of Heaven, came into being. She founded it around 1232, perhaps a year later. A richly decorated portal survives there to this day. Constance died in December 1240, and she was buried right there.[1][13]

7. Walls, silver and thin coins

Right after his father's death, Wenceslas I began fortifying Prague. The annalist writes that he had the town of Prague surrounded by a wall. And, with wood or stone, other market settlements too, which in our language are called towns.[1]

According to archaeologists, the wall of Prague's Old Town was more than two metres thick and about nine metres high. In front of it lay a moat up to twenty metres wide. About sixteen towers stood in the wall. It was completed in 1253.[1][12][20]

In 2017, archaeologists found one of its gates in Křižovnická Street. The paving of the road of that time lies about five metres below today's street.[20]

Towns were springing up all over the country at the time. Rulers invited settlers, among others from the Rhineland, and gave them rights based on German models, for example the law of Magdeburg. One charter from 1223, for example, gives the Moravian town of Uničov the same law that the burghers of Bruntál already have.[4][8]

Then silver was found. Archaeological finds suggest that mining in the Jihlava region probably began as early as the late 1230s. From the middle of the century, the Bohemian-Moravian Highlands, on the border between Bohemia and Moravia, went through their first great silver rush.[21]

At Staré Hory near Jihlava, archaeologists uncovered mine shafts, and next to them an ore-washing site: a system of channels and tanks in which the ore was washed.[22]

Jihlava had previously belonged to Constance's convent of Porta coeli. When mining began there, it passed back to the crown.[13][14]

Coins were struck from the silver. As early as the beginning of the century, bracteates began to be minted in Bohemia on the Meissen model. They were made of such thin sheet metal that they only had an image on one side. People called them plecháče, from the Czech word for sheet metal.[23]

But Wenceslas did not go down in history as a builder of towns. The annalist writes that he loved solitude. He lived with a few companions in small castles that he had built for himself. And during his reign, in 1245, tournaments were held in Bohemia for the first time.[1]

One such castle is Týřov, above the Berounka river. It first appears in the sources in 1249. For a long time, its ruins were thought to date from Wenceslas's time. But dendrochronology, dating by tree rings, shows that the palace only dates from the 1260s.[26]

8. The Mongols and a victory that never happened

In 1240, the annalist wrote down a single sentence: a great fear of the Tatars fell upon Bohemia.[1]

A year later, the Mongols, who were then called Tatars in Europe, invaded Hungary and Poland. The annalist writes that they killed the Polish duke Henry together with his army. It happened near Legnica in Silesia.[1]

Then they passed through Moravia. They entered it near Opava and left to the south-east, for Hungary. It was a swift passage. They did not besiege castles or towns. And to this day, archaeologists have not found clear evidence that they were here at all.[25]

But for a long time, a different version was told. The hero Jaroslav of Šternberk defeated the Tatars at Olomouc. That story is an invention.[10][25]

It grew gradually. Fourteenth-century chroniclers confused the year 1241 with the siege of Olomouc twelve years later. The chronicler Pulkava already tells of a sortie by a certain Šternberk. But the name Jaroslav was only given to him by Václav Hájek of Libočany, in 1541. And he was made famous by the poem Jaroslav in the Manuscript of Dvůr Králové. That is a forgery, which turned up in 1817.[10][25]

There really was fighting at Olomouc in 1253. Against the Hungarian army and the Cumans whom the Hungarians had brought with them. According to some historians, Zdeslav of Šternberk took part in the city's defence.[1][10][25]

And what about the year 1241 in Prague? The annalist noted that at the beginning of October, in the afternoon, there was an eclipse of the Sun, so that people thought it was dusk. According to astronomers, it was on 6 October.[1][29]

People thought it was dusk.
Prague Annals, under the year 1241, our translation

9. Father against son

In 1246, Duke Frederick of Austria fell in battle against the Hungarians. His niece Gertrude married Wenceslas's son Vladislav, who through her gained a claim to Austria. But he died just a year later.[1]

That left the younger son, Přemysl. And he turned against his father. On 31 July 1248, nobles in Prague accepted him as duke or king. And they swore allegiance to him without his father knowing anything about it.[1]

Without his father knowing anything at all.
History of King Wenceslas I, our translation from the Latin

Wenceslas returned with an army, and in August 1249 he took the Old Town. Then he besieged Prague Castle. For this, he brought in men from Jihlava. According to the editor, they were miners who knew how to build siege engines.[1]

At the Castle, water and fodder ran out. People and horses would soon have died. The king declared a truce, and the very next day he had himself solemnly crowned in the church of Saint Francis, in the convent of his sister Agnes. Then he held a feast.[1][18]

The son submitted. In tears, they kissed in reconciliation, and Přemysl received Moravia. But the king kept half of the Jihlava coinage for himself.[1]

On 20 August, Wenceslas entered the Castle on foot. The people and the nobility sang the hymn Hospodine, pomiluj ny, “Lord, have mercy on us”.[1]

A month later, Přemysl came to Týřov Castle for the pardon he had been promised. His father had him arrested there and sent to prison. The annalist comments dryly: utterly forgetful of his promise. He had the rebel nobles locked up in Prague in chains, two by two.[1]

Utterly forgetful of his promise.
History of King Wenceslas I, our translation from the Latin

In those years, the annalist also recorded oddities. In 1250, triangular hailstones reportedly fell and lay on the ground for seven days. And in December of that year, two ravens knocked a stone off the top of the prison in the tower by the bridge. It fell on the head of a knight named Pertold and killed him on the spot.[1]

But Wenceslas could not do without his son. In 1251, Austria submitted to the Bohemian king, and Přemysl rode into Vienna. In February 1252, he married Margaret, daughter of the former Austrian duke Leopold. She was a widow, and much older than he was.[1]

On 22 September 1253, Wenceslas I died. He was buried in the church of Saint Francis in the Convent of St Agnes. Archaeologists found his grave there in the twentieth century.[1][17][18]

Agnes outlived her brother by almost thirty years. She died in 1282 and was buried in the same convent, in the Chapel of the Virgin Mary.[6][11]

But her grave is empty. Archaeologists opened it in 1941 and found nothing in it. People had already searched for her remains in the seventeenth century, and again in 2010. Without success. The only known part of her remains is a lower jaw. For centuries, it was kept at the Escorial in Spain, and it returned to Bohemia in 1988. Agnes was declared a saint a year later, in November 1989.[6][11]

So in half a century, Bohemia became a hereditary kingdom. The bull from Basel confirmed it, and Wenceslas was the first king to inherit the crown from his father.[1][3][27]

On 17 October 1253, people came to welcome Přemysl to Prague as king, with great ceremony. A former rebel, and lord of Austria.[1] He is known as the Iron and Golden King.[28] He is the subject of the next episode.
