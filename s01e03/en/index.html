1. Amber from Mikulovice

Between 2007 and 2009, archaeologists excavated several groups of graves from the start of the Bronze Age at Mikulovice near Pardubice, in eastern Bohemia[3]. The team came from the Institute of Archaeology of the Czech Academy of Sciences in Prague and from the East Bohemian Museum in Pardubice[3]. In grave number 2 lay a woman who died at about thirty-five[3].

She had with her five bronze bracelets, three bronze pins and two gold ornaments that archaeologists call hair rings[3]. And above all a necklace of three strands of amber beads. There were over 400 of them[3][16]. The archaeologist Michal Ernée said of it that it is an absolutely unique piece of jewellery, with no parallel among the finds of its time[3].

It is an absolutely unique piece of jewellery, with no parallel among the finds of this period.
Michal Ernée, Institute of Archaeology of the Czech Academy of Sciences, press release of 24 May 2022

How old is the grave? Radiocarbon dating from a laboratory in Mannheim places it between 1880 and 1750 BC[3].

Amber is fossilised resin, and it does not occur naturally in our lands. Analyses show that the amber from Bohemian graves of that time is of Baltic origin[1][3]. So someone had to carry it across a large part of Europe.

DNA was recovered from one of the woman's bones. According to it she had fair skin, brown hair and brown eyes[3]. In 2022 the Moravian Museum in Brno completed a reconstruction of her appearance, based on the surviving skull and on these data[3].

In this episode we will cover almost eighteen centuries. We begin with the people who learned to cast bronze, and we end at the time when iron appears in our lands. We will see graves under mounds, fields full of urns, and the Býčí skála cave, which archaeologists have been arguing about for more than 150 years.

As last time, one note applies. From this period we have no written source from our lands. Everything rests on finds, on their dating, and on interpretation, which may change with further research.

We begin with the culture to which the woman from Mikulovice belonged.

2. The Únětice Culture

It is called the Únětice culture, after the village of Únětice near Prague. Some time at the end of the 1870s, Čeněk Rýzner, a doctor from Roztoky, the neighbouring town, excavated two cemeteries there[1][18]. He published the finds together with plans, and in some respects his work can still be used today[1]. But where exactly the two cemeteries lay, we no longer know[1]. The name Únětice culture was first used in 1892 by the anthropologist Jindřich Matiegka[1][19].

When did this culture exist? The Early Bronze Age begins in Central Europe in about the 23rd century BC[1]. It lasts six to seven hundred years, depending on where one places its end[1]. The Únětice culture flourished most at the beginning of the 2nd millennium BC, when its settlement of Bohemia was very dense[1].

Most of the finds come from the basins of the Elbe and the lower Vltava, from around Prague and from the Kolín region east of it[1]. But the Únětice culture was not only Bohemian. We know it from Moravia, and its influence reached into Germany, Poland, Austria and Slovakia[1].

How did these people live? Their main livelihood was farming[1]. They grew wheat, barley, rye, millet, peas and lentils, and they kept cattle, pigs, sheep and goats[1]. We know little about their villages. From the small cemeteries, archaeologists judge that three to four families lived in one settlement[1].

They laid their dead in the grave crouched on the right side, with the head to the south and the face to the east[1]. And here is a difference from the last episode. The Corded Ware and Bell Beaker cultures laid men and women in opposite ways. In Únětice graves everyone lies the same way, regardless of sex[1][4].

Were they descendants of the Bell Beaker people? Archaeology sees continuity. According to the more recent view, the Únětice culture grew out of the Bell Beaker milieu[1]. A genetic study of skeletons from Bohemia, published in 2021, showed however that it was not so simple[4]. At the start of the Únětice culture, new ancestry entered the local population. According to the authors it made up at least two fifths and ultimately came from somewhere in the north-east[4]. From where exactly, they cannot say[4]. They themselves point out that amber, too, came to Bohemia from the Baltic[4]. Yet the change cannot be seen in the graves or in the objects[4].

So once again, pots and genes do not always tell the same story.

But what sets the Únětice culture apart from earlier times most of all is metal.

3. Bronze and Long-Distance Routes

Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin[1]. The term Bronze Age was introduced by the Danish archaeologist Thomsen. That was in 1836, when he was arranging the collection of the museum in Copenhagen[1].

Bronze has a number of advantages over pure copper. It melts at a lower temperature, it casts better, and cold hammering makes it considerably harder[1]. It can be made into things that were not possible before: sickles, chisels, daggers, and later swords and helmets[1].

Where did the two raw materials come from? Copper and tin are mostly found far apart, so long-distance routes had to work[1]. Copper came to Bohemia from Alpine mines, according to the prevailing view from the basin of the river Salzach[1]. It travelled as ring ingots. These are rings of roughly hammered copper with bent ends[1]. One ingot weighs about 200 grams[1]. Some researchers therefore see them as a forerunner of money[1].

The route most likely led from the Danube across the Šumava mountains, over the Vyšší Brod pass, and on along the Vltava into central Bohemia[1]. It is along the Vltava that stores of ring ingots lie in the ground[1]. And in southern Bohemia, finds of Baltic amber cluster along the same route[1].

And tin? Here the evidence was missing for a long time. The Ore Mountains, on today's Czech–German border, are the obvious candidate. But a summary work by the Prague Institute of Archaeology from 2008 called Bronze Age mining there a hypothesis[1]. Twelve years later a study appeared from the site of Schellerhau on the Saxon side of the mountains[5]. According to it, tin ore from stream deposits was being worked there as early as the beginning of the 2nd millennium BC[5]. The authors add that until then, Bronze Age tin mining had not been confirmed anywhere in Europe[5].

What did the Únětice people give in return for copper? We do not know. Gold from the rivers of southern Bohemia has been suggested, or indeed tin, but convincing evidence is lacking[1].

Finished products often ended up in the ground. Archaeologists call such sets hoards[1]. One study records 117 of them from the Early Bronze Age in Bohemia[1]. Why did people bury them? They could have been offerings, or a reserve for worse times. There is no single answer[1].

One hoard comes from Kozí hřbety near Horoměřice, on the edge of Prague. In a cleft in the rock lay six daggers and an axe blade[1]. All were heavily worn, and most likely nobody was meant to take them out of the cleft again[1]. Yet such weapons marked the standing of the person who carried them[1].

And that brings us to the question of who made the decisions in that society.

4. The Rich and the Powerful

Most of the earlier Únětice graves are furnished in a similar way[1]. But some graves stand out. They contain a dagger, an axe or gold, and archaeologists regard their owners as leading figures of the community[1]. If the anthropological assessments are right, they were not only men[1].

The woman from Mikulovice belonged to that group as well. According to the archaeologists, hers is the richest female grave of the whole Únětice culture[3]. At the Mikulovice cemetery almost 900 amber objects were found[3]. An amber necklace lay in more than two fifths of the women's graves there[3]. No other site of the Únětice culture has yielded so much amber[3].

Did these people have princes? In Bohemia we have no evidence for that. The graves that are called princely lie elsewhere: under mounds in central Germany[1]. The best known is the mound at Leubingen. Oak timber from its grave has been dated by tree rings. The tree was felled around 1942 BC at the earliest, give or take ten years[1]. The wealth of those graves apparently came from the salt trade and from metalworking workshops[1]. The word prince, though, is only a working label used by archaeologists. The Czech summary work puts it in quotation marks[1].

The Únětice culture in Bohemia, according to archaeologists, rested mainly on well-developed farming[1].

At the end of the Early Bronze Age the picture changes. People found settlements on hilltops, some of them fortified with a ditch. Such settlements often guarded long-distance routes[1]. Burials directly in settlement pits become more frequent[1]. And in southern and western Bohemia, the burial mound takes hold above the graves[1].

It is the mound that gave the next period its name.

5. The Mounds

The Middle Bronze Age is called the Tumulus period. A tumulus, a burial mound, is a bank of earth or stones heaped over a grave[1]. At that time the custom linked a vast territory, from the Carpathian Basin to Alsace[1]. The impulse most likely came from the Carpathian Basin. How exactly it spread remains, according to archaeologists, a matter of speculation[1].

Archaeologists now place the beginnings of the Tumulus culture in Bohemia as early as the 17th century BC[1]. It ends in the 13th century BC[1]. So it lasted at least four hundred years, longer than was once thought[1].

Most mounds are known from southern and western Bohemia[1]. Some cemeteries have more than a hundred of them[1]. In the fertile regions of central Bohemia, most of them were probably destroyed by later farming[1].

One cemetery lies in the Sváreč wood near Žákava, in the Plzeň region of western Bohemia. Originally 92 mounds stood here[1]. Some of them were excavated back in the 19th century by a researcher named Franc[1]. Under one mound he found a pit with sherds, a whetstone and fragments of a mould for casting a small axe[1]. It was the first trace of a settlement of this culture[1].

Further settlements were a long time coming. Earlier researchers therefore took the people of the Tumulus culture for nomadic herders[1]. Today more than two hundred of their settlements are known from Bohemia. Finds of bronze sickles and storage pits speak of settled farmers[1].

How were the dead buried under the mounds? In both ways. Sometimes people buried the whole body; more often they burned it[1]. There was no strict rule[1]. And the size of a mound says nothing about the wealth of the person buried in it[1]. We recognise people of higher standing by what was placed with them, by gold jewellery or weapons[1].

Bronze changes too. There are more kinds of products, complex shapes appear, and so do new casting techniques[1].

Towards the end of this period, a place arose at Velim in the Kolín region that still puzzles archaeologists today.

6. Velim and the Urnfields

The hill is called Skalka. A large rescue excavation began here in 1984[1]. The archaeologists uncovered several systems of ditches. The inner one encloses an area about 160 metres across[1]. The outer one is more than three hundred metres across[1].

In the ditches and pits lay a great many human skeletons, together with pottery and with gold and bronze objects[1]. Most of the bones were scattered, in no order at all[17]. The site came to an end at the transition to the Urnfield period[1]. Radiocarbon dates place its destruction somewhere around 1400 BC[17].

What happened here? There are at least three interpretations. The first is a cult site. The Czech archaeologist Jiří Hrala saw human sacrifices here and also considered cannibalism[1][17]. The Czech summary work writes about them too, but at the same time admits that proving cannibalism is harder than it once seemed[1]. A different interpretation was offered by the German archaeologist Heidi Peter-Röcher[6]. According to her it was above all a burial place, where extensive funeral rites were held[6]. The dead include men, women and children of all age groups in normal proportions. In her view that is hard to square with sacrifice[6].

The third interpretation is war. The British archaeologist Anthony Harding, whose team also dug at Velim, rejects the idea of a cemetery[17]. According to him, complete skeletons and grave goods are missing[17]. In 2007 his team concluded that the fortified site was stormed by enemies and its inhabitants killed[17]. He himself adds that this cannot explain everything[17]. The Czech summary work concludes that the nature of the site has not yet been satisfactorily explained[1].

We know a similar place from Moravia too[1][6]. At the hillfort of Cezavy near Blučina, nearly two dozen bronze hoards and many human bones were found[7].

Around the middle of the 13th century BC the Late Bronze Age begins[1]. A large part of Europe then adopted the same custom. To burn the dead and place the ashes in a vessel, an urn, in a large shared cemetery[1]. Hence the name urnfields[1].

In Bohemia three cultures lived side by side. The Knovíz culture in central, southern and north-western Bohemia, the Milavče culture in the west, and the Lusatian culture in the north and east[1]. The boundary between the Knovíz and Lusatian cultures held for more than five hundred years[1]. The Knovíz culture is named after a site at Knovíz in the Kladno district, north-west of Prague[1].

Bronze production reached its peak at that time[1]. Hoards are also at their most numerous, and the most common object in them is the sickle[1].

The Knovíz culture has a darker side as well. In pits at its settlements, human skeletons and single bones are found, some with a smashed skull[1]. Earlier researchers saw this as clear proof of cannibalism. Today's authors are more cautious. In their view cannibalism probably did exist, but traces of cooking or of gnawing on the bones have been found only exceptionally[1]. What is certain is that some people at that time did not receive an ordinary burial[1].

At the end of the Bronze Age, fortified settlements rise on hilltops, and according to archaeologists a class of warriors begins to form[1]. In the richest Bohemian grave of that time, at Hostomice near Teplice in the north-west, lay a bronze sword. Beside it were four spearheads and a knife. All of them made of the new metal, iron[1].

That brings us to the age that took its name from iron.

7. Iron and Hallstatt

With the Hallstatt period, the Iron Age begins in Central Europe[2]. It is named after Hallstatt in the Austrian Salzkammergut. There are salt mines there, and a cemetery with richly furnished graves[2]. In Bohemia archaeologists count it from about 800 BC[2]. Some allow for a start half a century later[2].

Iron becomes a common material for tools and weapons, at first still alongside bronze[2]. But one thing is surprising. Iron objects are common in Bohemian graves and settlements from the very start of this period. And yet we know of no clear evidence from Bohemia that iron was also smelted from ore here[2].

Society changes too. Weapons, riding and the horse-drawn wagon become the marks of standing[2]. Goods and ideas flow into Central Europe from the south, mainly from the Etruscans and the Greeks[2].

In Bohemia two spheres meet at that time. Central and north-western Bohemia belong to the Bylany culture, western and southern Bohemia to the Hallstatt Tumulus culture[2]. The east and north of the country keep to the Urnfield tradition[2]. The Bylany culture lasts until the middle of the 6th century BC[2]. It is named after a cemetery at Bylany near Český Brod, east of Prague[2]. This is not the Bylany near Kutná Hora that we talked about last time.

Its richest graves have a timber chamber[2]. In some of them the mourners placed a whole four-wheeled wagon[2]. The wood has decayed. What survived are the iron tyres of the wheels, the fittings and the nails[2]. The wheels were more than 80 centimetres across[2]. With the wagon went horse harness and a yoke studded with bronze, like the one found at Hradenín in the Kolín region[2]. Rows of vessels stood in the grave. In the richest there were as many as seventy[2].

Who lay in those chambers? Archaeologists call these graves princely, but they themselves consider the label misleading[2]. Most likely these were chiefs of fairly small communities[2]. People buried with horse harness alone may have belonged to their retinue[2]. Strongly fortified hillforts were not yet being built in the earlier part of the Hallstatt period[2].

But the most famous Hallstatt-period find from the Czech lands does not lie in Bohemia. It lies in a cave in the Moravian Karst, north of Brno.

8. Býčí skála

The Býčí skála cave lies near Habrůvka in the Blansko district[10]. Its name means Bull Rock. Its entrance chamber is called the Entrance Hall, in Czech Předsíň. In 1869 two cousins from the nearby village of Olomučany found a small bronze figurine of a bull here[21]. The find brought in Jindřich Wankel, who is called the father of Moravian archaeology[9][21]. In the autumn of 1872 he dug in the Entrance Hall[9].

He found the remains of about forty people[8]. Around them lay bronze and pottery vessels, iron weapons, gold and bronze ornaments, thousands of glass and amber beads, and the remains of several wagons[8]. The finds, including the bull figurine, are now in the Natural History Museum in Vienna[12][13].

Wankel first described the find as a place of sacrifice[9]. Later he made it famous with a different interpretation. According to him a chieftain was buried here, and a retinue of young women accompanied him to the grave[9]. In doing so he left out the fact that there were men among the dead as well[9].

Since then the interpretations have come and gone. In the 1980s researchers proposed that the people in the cave were killed by an explosion[9]. According to later criticism, such an explosion was technically impossible[9]. At the end of the century the view prevailed that it was a place of sacrifice[9]. That is how the Vienna museum describes the find to this day[12]. The Archaeological Atlas of the Czech Republic writes that the interpretation is unclear and that a cult and sacrificial site is considered most often[8].

A new excavation came only in 2020 and 2021[10][11]. It was led by the archaeologist Martin Golec of Palacký University in Olomouc[14]. For the first time since Wankel's day, undisturbed layers from the Hallstatt period were documented[10]. It turned out that part of them are sandy deposits left by floods of the Jedovnice stream[11].

Golec's team draws a different picture from this[13][14]. In their view the cave was not the scene of a single mass sacrifice. It was a sanctuary in which the local elite laid their dead, generation after generation[13][14][15]. Golec dates it roughly between 575 and 450 BC[10][14]. The black layer that Wankel took for a trace of fire was, according to them, formed by decay[14].

For now it is the latest word, not the last. It rests on two seasons of excavation in a few small trenches[10][11]. According to the excavation report, the analyses of the samples taken are still being evaluated[10]. The whole story shows how much, with the very same bones, depends on the method and on the time in which a researcher interprets them.

Before we say goodbye, let us sum up what to remember from the Bronze Age and the Hallstatt period.

9. What to Remember

First: in our lands the Early Bronze Age is represented by the Únětice culture. It begins in about the 23rd century BC and lasts six to seven hundred years[1].

Second: bronze needs copper and tin, and those had to come from far away. Copper from the Alps, while Baltic amber travelled in the opposite direction. The woman from Mikulovice wore over 400 beads of it around her neck[1][3].

Third: we divide the Bronze Age into three stages according to how people buried their dead. First graves with crouched skeletons, then mounds, and finally fields of urns with ashes[1].

Fourth: around 800 BC the Iron Age begins. The richest people have themselves buried with a wagon[2].

And fifth: at neither Velim nor Býčí skála do archaeologists agree to this day on what exactly happened there[1][6][9][14][17].

Two questions remain open. Where exactly did the people come from who changed the local population at the start of the Bronze Age? And was Býčí skála a place of sacrifice, or a burial place? Further analyses may answer both.

The Greek historian Herodotus wrote in the 5th century BC that the river Istros, today's Danube, rises in the land of the Celts[20]. Archaeologists link the Celts with the La Tène culture, which in our lands follows the Hallstatt period[2]. That will be the next episode.
