1. The Grave at Mušov

In the autumn of 1988, a new road to the village of Pasohlávky was being built near Mušov in southern Moravia, in the east of today's Czech Republic. During the earthworks the builders came upon a grave by chance[9]. The excavation was led by the archaeologist Peška of the Regional Museum in Mikulov[9].

The archaeologists uncovered part of a large burial chamber. It measured about 6 by 4 metres and was originally covered by a structure of timber and stone[9]. Someone had robbed the grave long before them. Even so, almost 180 objects were left in it: silver and bronze vessels, glass, spearheads and arrowheads, shield fittings and sixteen spurs, some decorated with gold and silver[9].

The best known is a bronze cauldron. Its four ring handles are held by the heads of bearded men with their hair tied in a knot[9]. This is exactly the hairstyle that the Roman historian Tacitus described among the Suebi, a large group of Germanic tribes[4].

It is the mark of the tribe to comb the hair sideways and tie it up in a knot.
Tacitus, Germania 38

Who lay in the grave? According to anthropologists most likely three people, one of them a woman[9]. The latest items among the grave goods belong to the end of the 2nd century[9]. And only a kilometre and a half away, a fortress of the Roman army stood at that time[9]. Researchers therefore conclude that the man buried here was a Germanic ruler, most likely a king, who was backed by Rome[9]. That is an interpretation. We do not know his name.

What was a Germanic king doing with Roman silver in southern Moravia? And what was a Roman fortress doing here? That is what this episode is about. We will cover roughly six centuries: from the moment the Marcomanni came to Bohemia with King Maroboduus, through the wars with Emperor Marcus Aurelius, to the departure of the Lombards for Italy.

For the first time in this series we have written sources about events in our own lands[1]. But they were written by Romans and Greeks, that is, by neighbours and often enemies[1]. How the Germanic peoples saw the same events, we will not learn from them. So alongside the texts we will also read what archaeologists have found.

We begin at the turn of the era, in Bohemia.

2. Maroboduus and the Marcomanni

The last episode ended with the Celts. The Romans knew Bohemia by the name Boiohaemum[2]. At the end of the 1st century Tacitus wrote that this name, left by the Celtic Boii, had remained even though the inhabitants had changed[4].

The new inhabitants were Germanic. Archaeologists recognise them by different vessels, jewellery and graves. They call the oldest Germanic culture in Bohemia the Großromstedt culture[1]. It appeared here even before the event we know from written sources: the arrival of the Marcomanni[1].

The Marcomanni were led here by their leader Maroboduus. The Czech historian Dobiáš placed their arrival most likely between 9 and 6 BC[1]. At the latest, in his view, it was in 1 BC[1]. The summary work by Prague archaeologists calls Maroboduus the first historical figure of the Bohemian Basin[1]. He is the first person in this land we know by name.

The Greek geographer Strabo writes of him that as a young man he stayed in Rome and enjoyed the favour of Emperor Augustus[5]. But the most was recorded by Velleius Paterculus, a Roman officer and a contemporary of Maroboduus[1][2]. According to him, Maroboduus led his people out of Rome's reach into the plains surrounded by the Hercynian Forest[2]. He then subdued the neighbouring tribes by war or by treaties[2]. Velleius portrays him as a man of noble birth, strong and brave[2].

A barbarian by birth rather than by intellect.
Velleius Paterculus, Roman History II 108

How great was his power? Velleius writes that Maroboduus's army had 70,000 foot soldiers. With them, he says, went 4,000 horsemen[2]. That is the figure of a Roman author, not a census. Today's researchers think it rather tells us how many warriors Maroboduus could call up on occasion, allies included. Nor do they rule out that Velleius deliberately inflated the number of the enemy[1]. His work, after all, was meant to glorify Tiberius, the later emperor[1].

The same caution is needed with the term Maroboduus's realm. Older literature drew its borders from the Danube all the way to the Baltic[1]. According to the more recent summary work, the extent of Maroboduus's power practically cannot be determined, because there is no support for it in either the written or the archaeological sources[1]. What is certain is that its centre was Bohemia[1]. The summary work therefore speaks of a power structure or a tribal confederation, and of the so-called realm of Maroboduus[1]. It does not use the word state.

Rome did not leave such a neighbour in peace for long.

3. Rome against Maroboduus

In AD 6, Roman armies set out against Maroboduus from two sides[1][2]. One was to advance from the Rhine through the territory of the Chatti. The other was led by Tiberius himself from Carnuntum on the Danube[2]. Tacitus later has Maroboduus say that twelve legions stood against him[3].

According to Velleius, both armies were only about five days' march from the enemy[2]. But then a great revolt broke out in Pannonia, Roman territory on the middle Danube[2]. The expedition was called off, and there was no fighting at all[1]. Maroboduus did not exploit Rome's weakness. He negotiated terms under which he remained independent[1].

We know of the whole campaign only from Velleius. Some of today's historians therefore think he exaggerated it, and a few even think it never got beyond a plan[1].

Three years later, in AD 9, Germanic warriors led by Arminius destroyed three Roman legions in the Teutoburg Forest[1][3]. Arminius sent Maroboduus the head of the defeated commander Varus as a call to fight together. Maroboduus sent it on to Rome[1][2]. He did not join the uprising[1].

Between the two leaders it ended in war. They clashed in AD 17[1][3]. Even before the battle, two tribes went over from Maroboduus to Arminius, the Semnones and the Lombards[3]. Let us remember that second name; it will return at the end of the episode. According to Tacitus, the battle ended without a clear result. But Maroboduus withdrew first, and his people began to desert him[3].

Two years later the land was invaded by Catualda, a young nobleman whom Maroboduus had once driven out[1][3]. He won the leading men over to his side and took the royal seat and the stronghold beside it[3]. Tacitus adds an interesting detail: there were also traders from the Roman provinces there, who had settled in a foreign land for the sake of profit[3].

Maroboduus crossed the Danube and asked Emperor Tiberius for refuge[3]. Tiberius settled him in Ravenna[3].

For eighteen years he did not leave Italy, and he grew old, his fame much diminished by too great a love of life.
Tacitus, Annals II 63

And where did his seat stand? We do not know. It used to be looked for at the Celtic oppida, at Závist or at Stradonice. But in Maroboduus's time those had been abandoned for decades[1]. Today archaeologists think rather of places with rich finds from that period, such as Prague-Bubeneč, the Kolín region or Lovosice. Evidence is still lacking[1].

The chronicles have given us names and dates. But how the Germanic people in Bohemia lived is shown by the finds.

4. How the Germanic People Lived in Bohemia

There are almost a thousand sites in Bohemia from the Early Roman period, that is, roughly from the first two centuries AD[1].

These were not towns. The basis of settlement was the farmstead: an above-ground house built on posts, and beside it one to three sunken huts, smaller buildings dug into the ground[1]. Several such farmsteads made up a village[1]. The Germanic people did not build fortifications[1].

They mostly cremated their dead[1][10]. They placed the ashes in a pottery vessel, an urn, and added grave goods[1].

The best-known cemetery lies on the hill of Pičhora near Dobřichov, in the Kolín region east of Prague. It was discovered in 1896[1]. Today 160 graves of men, women and children can be documented there[1]. About a fifth of them contained weapons, and usually goods from Roman workshops as well[1]. The oldest graves belong to the first decades AD, that is, to the time of Maroboduus[1]. The archaeologist Eduard Droberjar thinks that the richest of them may have held members of Maroboduus's retinue. That is a hypothesis[1].

Roman goods are unusually plentiful in Bohemia at that time[1]. This fits Tacitus's report about the traders at Maroboduus's court[1][3].

After Maroboduus's fall, Bohemia disappears from the written sources for whole centuries[1]. The focus of events shifted south-east, to southern Moravia and south-western Slovakia, closer to the Danube[1]. According to archaeologists, the leading Marcomannic families moved there too[1].

Here Rome tried a different policy from campaigns. It settled the retinues of Maroboduus and Catualda beyond the Danube and gave them a king, Vannius of the Quadi[3]. Later it secured peace on the frontier by installing friendly kings itself[1]. Tacitus said so plainly about the Marcomanni and the Quadi[4].

But the strength and power of the kings come from Roman authority. We help them seldom with arms, more often with money.
Tacitus, Germania 42

For about a hundred and fifty years it worked. Then came a great war.

5. The Marcomannic Wars

We call it the Marcomannic Wars. They began in 166 under Emperor Marcus Aurelius and lasted fourteen years[1]. They are named after the Marcomanni, but many Germanic and non-Germanic tribes fought in them[1]. Besides the Marcomanni, these were mainly the Quadi and the Sarmatian Iazyges[6][15].

At first Rome was on the defensive. Its army was weakened by a plague epidemic[1]. Around 170 the Marcomanni and the Quadi broke through the frontier and pushed as far as north-eastern Italy, where they destroyed the town of Opitergium[15][20]. The ancient source does not give the exact year[20].

Then the war turned. From 172 to 175 Marcus Aurelius led a successful counter-offensive[1]. The fighting moved beyond the Danube, to the territory of today's Moravia, Slovakia and Lower Austria[15]. According to archaeologists in Brno, the Roman army controlled southern and central Moravia[10].

What the war looked like through Roman eyes is shown by the Column of Marcus Aurelius in Rome. Its reliefs depict scenes from the fighting on Germanic territory[1]. It is the victor's view.

And what remained in the ground? Even for a short stay, the Roman army built itself a fortified camp. Inside stood only tents[10]. Around it ran a ditch that is V-shaped in section, and it is by this ditch that archaeologists recognise the camps[14]. In the middle Danube region about thirty such camps are known today[10]. Many of them lie along the Morava and the Dyje, roughly a day's march apart[10]. The largest covered over 40 hectares, like the camp at Charvátská Nová Ves near Břeclav[8][10]. You cannot see it from the ground. Archaeologists discovered it only from the air[8].

Traces of camps reach far to the north: to Olomouc, to Hulín and to Jevíčko[10][19]. The camp at Jevíčko, discovered in 2016, is according to the archaeologist Eduard Droberjar the northernmost one known from these wars[19]. At Olomouc-Neředín a camp ditch was found in 2001 during road construction[13]. The pottery from its fill belongs to the end of the 2nd century or the beginning of the next[14].

But one place differs from the temporary camps. And it takes us back to the grave from the beginning.

6. The Roman Fortress at Mušov

It is the hill of Hradisko near Mušov, 80 kilometres north of the Danube at Vienna[10]. From the top there is a wide view over the floodplain of the Dyje and the Svratka, which was densely settled by Germanic people in the first centuries AD[10]. According to the Brno archaeologists, the Marcomanni had a centre of power here. That is one reason why the Romans chose the place[10][19].

The first excavations here, in 1926 to 1928, were led by Anton Gnirs, with the support of the office of President Masaryk[10]. He uncovered the foundations of two buildings: a dwelling house and baths. Both had underfloor heating[10]. The bricks and roof tiles bore the stamp of the Tenth Legion, which was based at Vindobona, today's Vienna[10].

Modern research began in 1984 and showed how extensive the site is[10]. The ramparts are now known over a length of more than two and a half kilometres. The area with Roman buildings is estimated at 30 to 35 hectares[10]. The rampart was faced with unfired bricks. According to the archaeologists, hundreds of thousands of them were needed, and that points to a fortress meant to serve for a long time[10].

Before today's water park was built, archaeologists then found the foundations of a timber building 71 metres long[10]. To judge by its ground plan, it was a military hospital. Fourteen such buildings are known from the whole Roman Empire, and only this one stood on enemy territory[10].

A report by the historian Cassius Dio fits this. He writes that among both the Marcomanni and the Quadi, 20,000 soldiers each were stationed in forts[6]. They are said not to have let the Germanic people graze their cattle or till their fields in peace[6]. The soldiers themselves did not go short[6].

They even had baths and everything they needed in abundance.
Cassius Dio, Roman History LXXII (LXXI) 20

The number of soldiers is again the figure of an ancient author. What is more, the surviving Greek text is damaged at exactly that point[21]. But the baths at Mušov are real.

And now back to the royal grave. Its latest objects date from the time of these wars[9]. Alongside Germanic weapons it held things of the kind placed in graves in the Roman provinces: a lamp, a small table, medical instruments[9]. Some of the silver and bronze tableware was already more than a hundred years old at the time of the burial[9]. Researchers infer from this that these were gifts from the Roman authorities to a Germanic ally. In their view the man buried there was most likely a king installed by Rome during the wars[9]. We cannot be certain.

Since 2020 a visitor centre has stood below Hradisko[18].

The Romans were clearly preparing to stay. How far did their plans reach?

7. The Inscription at Trenčín and the End of the War

One answer can be found in Slovakia. On the castle rock in Trenčín a Latin inscription is carved to this day[15]. It says that a detachment of 855 soldiers of the Second Auxiliary Legion stayed here under the command of a man named Maximianus[15]. The inscription also gives the name of the place at that time: the place was called Laugaricio[15]. Who Maximianus was, and when his soldiers wintered here, we know from another inscription, found as far away as Algeria[15]. It was the winter of 179 to 180[15]. Trenčín lies outside today's Czechia. But it shows how deep in Germanic territory Roman garrisons then stood.

Did Marcus Aurelius want to turn these lands into provinces? The Historia Augusta says so explicitly; it is a collection of imperial biographies[7]. Cassius Dio writes only that the emperor would have subdued the whole region had he lived longer[6]. The Brno archaeologists consider it likely that Marcus Aurelius wanted to annex the Marcomannic territory permanently[10]. It is not certain.

He wished to make Marcomannia a province, and Sarmatia too.
Historia Augusta, Marcus Aurelius 24, 5

The emperor died on 17 March 180[6]. His son Commodus soon made peace[1][6]. According to Cassius Dio, the Marcomanni were allowed to assemble only once a month, in one place and under the supervision of a Roman centurion[6]. In return Rome evacuated the forts in their country[6]. The soldiers went back across the Danube[10].

At Mušov one person was left behind. Inside the fortifications archaeologists found the skeleton of a young man, laid in a pit without a proper burial[10]. Radiocarbon dating places his death between 130 and 210[10]. Analysis of DNA and isotopes suggests that he may have come from the Mediterranean[10]. If that is so, he would be the first person from the Roman Empire whom we know in Moravia[10].

And Bohemia? The summary work on the Roman period in Bohemia, from 2008, states that not a single piece of evidence for the presence of the Roman army has been found there[1]. Not even the later discovery at Jevíčko changes that. Jevíčko today belongs to the Pardubice Region, but it lies in historical Moravia[19]. This is the state of knowledge, not a final verdict.

The Roman frontier stayed on the Danube. Two centuries later the whole of Europe was set in motion.

8. The Migration Period and the Lombards

That age is what we call the Migration Period. In Bohemia it means the time from the end of the 4th century to the end of the 6th[1].

At its beginning stands the invasion of the Huns, nomads from the east[1]. Their power in Europe ended soon after the death of King Attila, roughly in the middle of the 5th century[1]. At the beginning of that century, Rome had ceded one of its Danubian provinces to them[1].

Bohemia lies far from the Danube, but the changes reached it. For the 5th century, archaeologists distinguish here the so-called Vinařice group, named after Vinařice in the Kladno district[1]. Rare glass and other luxury goods from the Rhineland show that a class of people lived here with contacts far to the west[1].

At the end of the 5th century a new wave of Germanic people comes to Bohemia from the north-west[1]. Among them were Thuringians and Lombards, the tribe that had once deserted Maroboduus[1][3]. How many such waves there were, and who predominated in which, is something researchers argue about[1].

The Lombards moved on towards the Danube. At the beginning of the 6th century they defeated the neighbouring Heruli and took control of the region north of the Danube, including southern Moravia[1][11]. Under King Wacho their power reached from Bohemia as far as today's Hungary[1].

In Moravia they left mainly cemeteries. We know almost no settlements from that time[11]. A large cemetery again lies at Mušov. The Brno archaeologists describe it as the central Lombard necropolis and estimate about 350 graves there[12]. More than two hundred and fifty of them have already been excavated[12]. They belong to the first half of the 6th century[12]. They include shallow graves with almost no goods as well as timber chambers with rich offerings[12]. Two silver coins of Ostrogothic kings, struck in Ravenna, were also found here[12]. The red stones in the brooches were, according to researchers, imported from as far away as India[11]. Many of the graves, however, were robbed long ago[12].

The chamber graves on the hill of Žuráň near Brno also belong to the Migration Period. According to archaeologists, people of the highest Germanic aristocracy apparently lay in them[16]. From the same hill, much later, Napoleon directed the Battle of Austerlitz[16].

What will DNA tell us about these people? Bones from the Moravian cemeteries are being studied in the international project HistoGenes. According to the Brno institute, the results are still being prepared for publication[12].

Then the Lombards left. Most of the tribe first moved to Pannonia, in today's Hungary[1][11]. Exactly when is disputed[1]. Their later chronicler Paul the Deacon recorded that from there they set out for Italy with their wives, children and all their possessions[17]. Paul also adds the year 568 from the Incarnation of the Lord[17]. According to him, Easter fell on the first of April that year, so the Lombards set out on the second[17]. Today's literature gives the same year[1][11]. According to Paul, they left Pannonia to allies whom he calls Huns[17]. Elsewhere he himself explains that this was what the Avars were called at first[17].

They set out from it in the month of April, in the first indiction, on the day after holy Easter.
Paul the Deacon, History of the Lombards II 7

The survey of Bohemia places the end of the Migration Period in the second half of the 6th century. Most of the Germanic inhabitants then leave for the Danube, and some go on to Italy[1]. At the same time, the first Slavs gradually make their way into our lands[1].

Before we come to them, let us sum up those six centuries.

9. What to Remember

First: shortly before the turn of the era, Maroboduus brought the Marcomanni to Bohemia. He is the first figure in this land we know by name. How far his power reached cannot be determined[1][2].

Second: only Romans and Greeks write about the Germanic peoples. Numbers such as Velleius's 70,000 foot soldiers are an author's figures, not a measured reality[1][2].

Third: during the Marcomannic Wars, which ended in 180, the Roman army stood in Moravia. At Mušov it built a fortress with baths and a hospital[1][10].

Fourth: in Bohemia no evidence of the Roman army has been found. Traces of camps reach to Olomouc and Jevíčko, and the inscription at Trenčín lies in Slovakia[1][10][15][19].

And fifth: in the 6th century our lands belonged, according to the summary work, to the realm of the Lombards[1]. In 568 they left for Italy[1][17].

Three questions remain open. Where did Maroboduus's seat stand? Who was the man from the royal grave at Mušov? And did any of the Germanic people stay here after the Lombards left? We will begin with that last one next time, with the first Slavs.
