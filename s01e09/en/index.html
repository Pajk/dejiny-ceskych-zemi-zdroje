1. The chain at Schweinfurt

A young duke arrives with his retinue at a convent in Schweinfurt, in today's Bavaria. He claims he is on his way to the emperor. In the evening, while the nuns are singing vespers, he seizes the most beautiful of the girls in their care, the daughter of a local count, and runs with her to his horse.[1]

But a chain of iron is stretched across the gate. The duke cuts through it with his sword as if it were a blade of straw. And the chronicler adds that the cut can still be seen today.[1]

The cut can still be seen today.
Cosmas, Chronicle of the Bohemians I.40, our translation from the Latin

The duke's name is Břetislav, and the girl he carried off is Judith. Cosmas quotes a line from Virgil here: love conquers all. And he dates the story to 1021.[1][9]

That chronicler is Cosmas, dean of the cathedral chapter in Prague. He was born around 1045. He wrote his chronicle as an old man, in the last years of his life. He will be our guide through this whole episode. Where he tells of his own time, he was often an eyewitness. Where he tells of the distant past, he retold what had been handed down.[1][3][12]

This episode covers two hundred years, in which a handful of strongholds around Prague grew into a state. We begin in the chaos around the year one thousand. Then come Oldřich and Moravia, Břetislav and his raid on Gniezno, Vratislav, the first Czech king, bloody struggles for the throne, the battle of Chlumec, and the second crown, that of Vladislav II. And we end in 1197, when two brothers finally share power by agreement.[1][5][11]

2. Prague in Polish hands

At the end of the last episode, the Přemyslids were left as the only rulers in Bohemia. But right at the start of the new millennium, they almost destroyed one another.[1][2]

The most detailed account comes from Thietmar, bishop of Merseburg in Saxony and a contemporary of these events. According to him, Duke Boleslaus III had his brother Jaromír castrated and wanted the younger Oldřich suffocated in a bath. Both brothers fled to Bavaria with their mother.[2][6]

Then Vladivoj, backed by Poland, came to the throne. Thietmar gives him a single sentence: he could never last even an hour without a drink. Vladivoj soon died, and Boleslaus returned.[2][6]

He could never last a single hour without drinking.
Thietmar of Merseburg, Chronicle V.23, our translation from the Latin

He returned in anger. Thietmar describes how, during Lent, in front of the assembled nobles, he killed his own son-in-law with a sword. The frightened nobles then sent for the Polish duke, Bolesław the Brave. Bolesław invited the Czech Boleslaus to talks, received him as a friend, and that night had his eyes put out. This happened in 1003.[2][6]

The Polish duke then rode into Prague himself and held Bohemia for a year.[2][6]

But he was opposed by the German king, Henry II. When Bolesław was told over supper that the king's army was approaching, he laughed: if they crawled like frogs, they could be here by now.[2]

If they crawled like frogs, they could already be here.
Thietmar of Merseburg, Chronicle VI.10, our translation from the Latin

That same night he heard bells from Vyšehrad, Prague's second castle, calling to arms. Bolesław the Brave fled from Prague. That was in 1004. During the flight, Soběbor, a brother of Saint Adalbert, died on the bridge. He had marched with the Poles.[2]

A hundred years later, Cosmas tells the story of that night more colourfully. Young Oldřich supposedly sent a man up the hill of Žižkov, and he blew a trumpet and shouted: They flee, they flee, the Poles! And the Poles ran away naked, some of them even without trousers, and fell from the broken bridge. Thietmar knows nothing about the trumpeter; that is Cosmas's version.[1]

And so the brothers whom Boleslaus III had once driven out returned to the throne of Prague.[1][2]

3. Oldřich, Božena and Moravia

Even then there was no peace. According to Cosmas, Jaromír was ambushed while hunting by nobles of the Vršovci clan. They stripped him, pinned him to the ground with stakes and jumped over him on horseback. He was saved by a servant, Dovora, who ran to Prague for help.[1]

In the end Oldřich ruled alone, and according to Cosmas he had Jaromír blinded. Cosmas tells the most famous love story in Czech history about him. While hunting, Oldřich saw a girl at a well, washing clothes. She was said to be whiter than snow and more beautiful than a sapphire. Her name was Božena, and she became the mother of Břetislav.[1]

Cosmas remarks that in those days a man could take two or three wives. It is a legend, but a beautiful one.[1]

More important than love was Moravia, the eastern part of today's Czech Republic. Oldřich drove the Poles out of it and handed it over to his son Břetislav. According to Cosmas, captured Poles were sold by the hundred, in chains, to Hungary. Historians disagree about exactly when Moravia was permanently joined to Bohemia. They place it between 1019 and 1029. Since then, the two lands have belonged together.[1][9]

Cosmas also knows lighter moments at Oldřich's court. On every hunt, a young courtier named Šebíř was the first to cut off the tail of the slain boar, prepare it and present it to the duke. Oldřich supposedly said: Šebíř, for this delicacy you deserve a bishopric. And Šebíř really did become bishop of Prague.[1]

Under Oldřich and Břetislav, a network of administrative castles also took shape. According to archaeologists there were roughly fifteen of them, and many later became towns, such as Litoměřice, Žatec or Čáslav.[25]

And both dukes minted a great many coins. At Želčany, in the Plzeň region of western Bohemia, a hoard was found, and in 1884 the National Museum bought more than two thousand pieces from it. Eight hundred and six of them were denarii of Oldřich.[24]

Oldřich died in 1034. Cosmas then describes how the blinded Jaromír himself seated Břetislav on the ducal throne. Ten thousand coins were thrown from the gallery, so that the crowd would not press around the duke, and the people cried “Krleš”. Shortly afterwards, Jaromír was stabbed in the privy by a henchman of one of the Vršovci.[1][9]

Břetislav, the abductor of Judith, now ruled both Bohemia and Moravia. And soon he marched north.[1]

4. Břetislav in Gniezno

In 1039, Břetislav marched on Poland. Cosmas even describes how he called up his army. He sent a rope of bast around the country with a message: anyone who fails to come will be hanged on one just like it.[1]

The goal was Gniezno, in today's Poland, where Saint Adalbert lay buried. He was the bishop of Prague killed by the Prussians, as we saw in the last episode. The Czechs wanted to bring him home. Cosmas writes that when they tried to break into the tomb without permission, they were struck dumb and blind for three hours. Only after a three-day fast were they allowed to touch Adalbert's body.[1]

Then came the moment Cosmas describes in the greatest detail. The duke stepped up to the pulpit, held his hand over Adalbert's tomb and proclaimed laws for the whole land. The Gniezno decrees. Whoever keeps a tavern shall be tied to a stake in the marketplace and beaten until the bailiff grows tired. And the beer shall be poured out on the ground.[1]

Beat him until the bailiff grows weary of it.
Cosmas, Chronicle of the Bohemians II.4, our translation from the Latin

He also banned Sunday markets and burials in fields outside cemeteries. After every point, Bishop Šebíř replied: cursed be whoever breaks it.[1]

Along with Adalbert, the Czechs carried off plunder. A golden cross, for which the Polish duke Mieszko had supposedly given three times his own weight in gold. More than a hundred wagons of bells and treasure. And behind them walked Polish nobles in chains. The procession reached Prague in August of the same year.[1]

For this robbery, the pope gave Břetislav a penance: to found and endow a church. That is how the chapter at Stará Boleslav came about. Much harsher was the response of King Henry III. He wanted the plunder for himself. The Czechs objected that they owed only what they had always paid. According to Cosmas, the king replied that the law has a nose of wax, but the king has a hand of iron, and a long one.[1]

The law has a nose of wax, but the king has a hand of iron, and a long one.
Cosmas, Chronicle of the Bohemians II.8, our translation from the Latin

In 1040, he invaded Bohemia. Cosmas describes how the king sat on a hill in the middle of the Bohemian Forest and mocked the Czechs for hiding like field mice in their holes. But his heavily armoured troops wore themselves out on the wooded slopes, and the Czechs charged at them from behind their barricades. According to Cosmas, never before had so many German nobles fallen, and the king himself escaped only because he had a horse at hand. A year later, however, the king returned and besieged Prague. Bishop Šebíř, the man with the boar's tail, slipped away at night to the royal camp. In the end, Břetislav bought peace for fifteen hundred marks of silver. Cosmas summed it up in verse: queen money quenched the king's anger.[1]

On his deathbed in Chrudim, in eastern Bohemia, Břetislav then voiced an idea that was meant to prevent further killings within the family: the eldest of the dynasty should always rule. Cosmas puts a question into his mouth: what did two brothers do, and what will five do? It was not a written law, rather the advice of a dying father.[1]

Around 1050, Břetislav also reformed the coinage. Břetislav's older denarii from the hoard at Levý Hradec weigh about one gram and contain over ninety per cent silver. Someone buried them there without a pot, most likely just in a cloth or leather pouch.[24]

Břetislav died in 1055. His eldest son took the throne.[1]

5. Sázava and the Slavonic monks

That son was called Spytihněv, and Cosmas captured his character in a single anecdote. As a young man, while the walls of Prague Castle were being built, he knocked the oven of the abbess of St George's into the ditch. He supposedly laughed that today the lady abbess would not be enjoying any warm cakes. As duke, he later drove her out.[1]

Under Spytihněv, one exceptional monastery also came to an end for a time. It was founded on the Sázava river, southeast of Prague, by the hermit Procopius. According to legend, Duke Oldřich found him there while chasing a stag on a hunt.[1][4]

Archaeologists who excavated at Sázava for twenty-seven seasons found that in the 11th century the monastery was built of wood.[23][28]

At Sázava, Mass was celebrated in Old Church Slavonic, the language that Constantine and Methodius had brought to Central Europe. The monks copied Slavonic books here. Procopius died in 1053. Soon afterwards, Spytihněv drove the Slavonic monks out to Hungary.[1][4]

It was his brother Vratislav who brought them back. The Sázava abbot Božetěch was a painter and woodcarver, and Vratislav favoured him so much that once Božetěch himself placed the crown on his head. But only a bishop was allowed to do that. As a penance, he had to carve a crucifix as large as himself and carry it on his back to Rome. And he really did.[4][8]

At the turn of 1096 and 1097, Slavonic Sázava came to an end for good. The monks were driven out, and their chronicler writes that the books of their language were destroyed and would never again be read in that place. The new Latin abbot found the monastery so plundered that it could not have fed the brothers for even a month. Yet the first part of the stone church had been consecrated only a year before.[4][8][23]

The books of their language were destroyed and will never again be read in that place.
The Monk of Sázava, our translation from the Latin

Božetěch's crown, however, takes us back a little. To how Vratislav came to wear a crown at all.[1][4]

6. Vratislav, the first king

Vratislav II was a long-standing ally of Emperor Henry IV. For his loyalty he received a reward that no Přemyslid before him had: the title of king.[1][14]

It was granted to him in Mainz. The coronation took place in Prague on 15 June, and he was anointed by Archbishop Egilbert of Trier. Crowned with him was his wife Svatava, the first Czech queen. Cosmas dates the ceremony to 1086. Some historians place it a year earlier.[1][14]

Cosmas was about forty at the time. Beneath his copy of an imperial charter for the bishopric of Prague, he noted: I saw the emperor himself signing it with his own hand.[1]

I saw the emperor himself signing it with his own hands.
Cosmas, Chronicle of the Bohemians II.37, our translation from the Latin

At the coronation, clergy and nobles cried out three times: To Vratislav, king of Bohemia and of Poland, great and peaceful, crowned by God, life, health and victory! The Polish title, however, appears only in Cosmas, and historians doubt it.[1]

Vratislav and his immediate successors lived mainly at Vyšehrad, not at Prague Castle. Around 1070, Vratislav founded a chapter there, with the church of Saints Peter and Paul. A stone passage on an arch across the moat connected it to the Vyšehrad acropolis, and part of it can still be seen today. The Rotunda of St Martin, which still stands at Vyšehrad, also dates from the 11th or early 12th century.[15][17]

Vratislav's coronation is also linked to a famous illuminated manuscript, the Vyšehrad Codex. Today it is kept by the National Library in Prague.[27]

The royal title, however, was not hereditary. It was a personal gift from the emperor. Vratislav died in 1092, and his successors were once again only dukes.[1][7][8]

And among those dukes a struggle broke out that Cosmas saw with his own eyes.[1]

7. Cosmas and the massacre of the Vršovci

Břetislav's rule about the eldest of the dynasty sounded simple. In practice, cousins and uncles fought over the throne, and each had his allies among the nobles. The most powerful of them were the Vršovci.[1][7]

In 1108, Duke Svatopluk had them massacred. Their head, Mutina, was beheaded in the middle of an assembly in Wrocław, in Silesia. At Libice, Božej was killed together with his son. In Prague, people were slaughtered in the marketplace and on Petřín hill.[1]

Cosmas writes here as an eyewitness. We saw them being wretchedly dragged to the marketplace, crying: Mother, mother! How many heads fell, he says, he was unable to find out.[1]

We saw them dragged to the marketplace, crying: Mother! Mother!
Cosmas, Chronicle of the Bohemians III.24, our translation from the Latin

The dukes fared no better. Whoever lost ended up blinded, in prison or in exile. Bořivoj II was arrested in 1110 and spent almost seven years as a prisoner in Hammerstein Castle on the Rhine.[7]

His half-brother Břetislav II had already died in 1100 and was buried in St Vitus's. That tomb still held a surprise in our own century. When archaeologists opened it in 2002, they found not a man's skeleton, but the bones of children and women. Over the centuries, the remains of the Přemyslids were moved and mixed up several times.[7][26]

And Cosmas himself was growing old. He had once studied in Liège, in present-day Belgium, and now he was writing the first great chronicle of Bohemia. He was married, as priests often still were at that time. His wife Božetěcha died in January 1117, and he wrote of her: my faithful companion in all things.[1][12]

He writes about himself in old age with humour. His voice, he says, now hisses hoarsely like a goose's, and soft bread with a toasted crust pleases him more than learned disputes. He died in October 1125. His chronicle was then continued by others, the Canon of Vyšehrad and the Monk of Sázava. And the very next winter, they recorded a great victory.[1][3][12]

8. Chlumec and the lance of Saint Wenceslas

While Cosmas was still alive, in 1125, Soběslav I became duke. His rival, Otto of Olomouc, turned to the German king Lothair. Otto supposedly told him that one could ride into Bohemia as if going hunting, with falcons and hawks.[3][4]

Soběslav sent word to Lothair that the choice of the Czech duke lay in the hands of the Czech nobles, and the emperor merely confirmed it. Lothair marched anyway, in February 1126, across the Ore Mountains on Bohemia's northern border. In the deep snow, his horsemen had to go on foot. At Chlumec, the Czechs were waiting for them.[4]

The Canon of Vyšehrad recorded what was told about the battle. The chaplain Vitus stood in armour and helmet like Achilles, holding the lance of Saint Wenceslas. On the lance hung the banner of Saint Adalbert, which had supposedly been found in the wall of a village church. In tears, the chaplain cried out that he could see Saint Wenceslas on a white horse above the tip of the lance, fighting for them.[3]

I see Saint Wenceslas on a white horse above the tip of the holy lance, fighting for us.
Canon of Vyšehrad, for the year 1126, our translation from the Latin

It is a miracle from a chronicle, but the battle itself is certain. On 18 February 1126, Soběslav defeated the king. According to the canon, five hundred leading Saxons fell, and only three Czechs. Otto of Olomouc fell too.[3][4]

That same year, Soběslav restored the chapel on Říp Mountain.[3]

In those years, Prague Castle received a new wall. After 1135, the older wall of rubble stone bound with clay was replaced by a wall of small blocks of marlstone, for the first time laid in mortar. A section of it can still be seen in the basement of the Imperial Stables.[18]

The paintings in the rotunda at Znojmo, in southern Moravia, date from the same period. The whole line of succession of the ruling family is painted on its walls. An inscription that someone scratched into the plaster refers to the year 1134.[22]

But Soběslav was harsh at home too. When he uncovered a conspiracy in 1130, he had the conspirators beheaded in the Prague marketplace. He had his nephew Břetislav blinded.[4]

The Přemyslid state could now defeat even a king. But the next Přemyslid chose to help the king instead.[3][11]

9. The second crown and one spirit

Vladislav II promised Emperor Frederick Barbarossa help against Milan. And he got a crown. At the imperial diet in Regensburg, on 11 January 1158.[11]

A week later, the emperor issued a charter allowing Vladislav to wear a royal diadem on the greatest feast days. The Czech National Archives call it the oldest surviving charter that documents the existence of the Czech state. And in September, when Milan surrendered, the emperor placed the crown on the Czech king's head once again, before the eyes of the army.[11]

Under Vladislav, a stone bridge across the Vltava began to be built in Prague. It was named after Queen Judith and stood where the Charles Bridge stands today. The chronicler Vincent of Prague writes that it was built in three years. Researchers consider that an exaggeration, but by the end of the 1160s at the latest, the bridge was standing.[10][20]

It was the first stone bridge across the Vltava. According to a reconstruction it was more than five hundred metres long and stood on twenty piers. In the Kotlář House in Prague's Lesser Town, you can still see its paving, with ruts worn by wagon wheels. It served for more than a hundred and seventy years, until a flood swept it away in 1342.[19][20]

Vladislav also founded the monastery at Strahov, where he brought the Premonstratensians.[21]

Then the Přemyslids began quarrelling over the throne again, one after another. Abbot Jarloch of Milevsko describes how in 1182 the Czech nobles drove out Duke Bedřich and elected Conrad Otto from Moravia.[5]

The emperor summoned them to Regensburg. He wanted to frighten the nobles who came. He had a great many axes brought in, as if he meant to have them beheaded. The nobles fell at his feet and begged for mercy. Bohemia went back to Bedřich, and the emperor ordered Conrad Otto to be content with Moravia.[5]

He had a great many axes brought in, as if he meant to have them beheaded.
Jarloch of Milevsko, for the year 1182, our translation from the Latin

In 1187, the Church entered the disputes too. Before the emperor, Duke Bedřich declared that the bishop of Prague was his chaplain. The princes of the Empire ruled the opposite: the bishop was subject only to the emperor. And he received a charter with a golden seal to prove it.[5]

That bishop was called Henry Břetislav, and at the end of the century he also became duke. He ruled Bohemia as bishop and duke at the same time. After his death, Jarloch called him the golden flower of Bohemia. When he died in June 1197, the nobles released Vladislav Henry from his prison and elected him duke. His brother Přemysl would not accept this, and in the autumn he marched on Prague with an army.[5]

And then something happened that we have not yet seen in this episode. The brothers did not fight. On 6 December 1197, they reached an agreement: Přemysl would rule in Bohemia, Vladislav in Moravia. Jarloch recorded it in these words: as one spirit, so one principality.[5]

As one spirit, so one principality.
Jarloch of Milevsko, for the year 1197, our translation from the Latin

In two hundred years, a shattered duchy had thus become a state of Bohemia and Moravia, with two crowned rulers behind it and a chronicle that described it.[1][5][11]

In less than a year, Přemysl will receive a royal crown. And this time he will win it for good, for the whole dynasty.[5][16] That is the subject of the next episode: The Golden Bull of Sicily.
