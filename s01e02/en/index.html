1. The Well from Ostrov

In 2018 a motorway was about to be built near the village of Ostrov in eastern Bohemia, in today's Czech Republic. During a rescue excavation there, archaeologists from the Archaeological Centre Olomouc came upon a wooden well[12][13]. At the corners stood four oak posts with grooves cut into them, and planks were slotted into the grooves[12]. The ground plan measures 80 by 80 centimetres. It is about 140 centimetres high[13][15].

How old is it? Specialists from Mendel University in Brno measured the tree rings. The trees for the planks were felled at the turn of 5256 and 5255 BC[12][20]. The corner posts come from a trunk cut down about three years earlier[12]. According to the authors of the study, it is the oldest wooden structure in the world that has been dated by tree rings[15]. The director of the Olomouc centre himself added that such a record will most likely be temporary[13]. Older wells whose rings cannot be measured may well exist[13].

The carpenters who built the well did not know metal. They worked with tools of stone, bone, horn or wood[13]. The wood survived for more than seven thousand years, completely waterlogged. Conservators from the University of Pardubice therefore preserved it with a sugar solution, and today it belongs to the collections of the East Bohemian Museum in Pardubice[12][14].

Who were the people who built that well? They were among the first farmers in our lands. In this episode we will cover roughly three thousand years: from the first fields and longhouses, through the large circular structures we call rondels, to the first copper, the plough and the wagon. And we will look at what their DNA says about where these people came from, and what it does not say.

One note in advance. From this time we have no written source at all. Everything you will hear rests on finds, on their dating, and on interpretation, which may change with further research.

We begin with the question of where farming in Central Europe came from.

2. Where Farming Came From

Before the farmers, hunters and gatherers lived in our lands. Archaeologists call their time the Mesolithic, the Middle Stone Age[1].

Farming arose elsewhere: in the Near East, where people moved from gathering to growing cereals and keeping animals[1]. From there the new way of life spread through Anatolia and the Balkans into the Carpathian Basin. It went on along the great rivers: up the Danube and the Tisza, north along the Morava, the Oder and the Vistula, west to the Elbe and the Rhine[1].

It reached Central Europe with a culture that archaeologists named after the decoration of its vessels: the Linear Pottery culture. What those people called themselves we do not know. Calibrated radiocarbon dates place this culture roughly between 5600 and 5000 BC[1]. Radiocarbon dating always gives a range, not an exact year[1]. Only tree rings can give the exact year a tree was felled, as with the well from Ostrov[5].

And now the main dispute. Did new people come to Central Europe, or did local hunters learn to farm? The older view assumed colonisation from the south-east[1]. At the end of the 20th century some researchers swung to the opposite view. Genetic studies of that time were based on the DNA of today's Europeans and suggested that the share of newcomers was small[1][8]. As late as 2007, a summary work on the Bohemian Neolithic therefore wrote that the role of the local hunters needed to be reassessed[1].

Then it became possible to read DNA straight from prehistoric bones. A study from 2015 showed that the first farmers in Germany, Hungary and Spain were closely related to one another and differed from the local hunters and gatherers[17]. According to more recent work they descended from migrants from Anatolia and were genetically almost indistinguishable from the farmers there[16][19]. For people of the Linear Pottery culture from Germany, the share of ancestry from local hunters came out at only four to five per cent[19]. In the following millennia that share grew[16][19].

So today the prevailing conclusion is that farming was brought to Central Europe mainly by incoming people[16][17][19]. It rests mainly on skeletons from Germany, Hungary and Spain[17][19]. But DNA will not tell us what language they spoke or what their encounters with the hunters looked like. And every study rests only on the skeletons that have survived and that someone has examined.

How these people lived was shown best by one excavation near Kutná Hora in central Bohemia.

3. The People of the Longhouses

In 1953 the archaeologist Bohumil Soudský began to dig at Bylany near Kutná Hora. The excavation lasted until 1967[3]. The archaeologists uncovered more than seven hectares and identified the ground plans of almost 150 longhouses[3].

That does not mean a large settlement stood here. The village existed for about five centuries, until the end of the 6th millennium BC, and the houses were replaced over time. At any one moment there were at least two or three of them, at most eleven[3].

All that remains of the houses are the traces of posts set into the ground. From them we know that the frame consisted of five rows of posts: the inner ones carried the roof, the outer ones formed the base of the clay walls[1]. What the house looked like above the ground is already a reconstruction. It is based on the ground plans and on buildings known from ethnography. Researchers argue, for example, about whether the floor was on the ground or raised[4].

What did these people grow? In Bohemia mainly emmer wheat, apparently together with einkorn. Peas, lentils and flax are also attested[1]. We should not imagine the fields as today's wide expanses. According to one model they were rather small, carefully tended plots resembling gardens, where about one hectare was enough for a family. A plough drawn by cattle is not attested in the Neolithic[1].

For working wood there was a typical polished stone tool, the shoe-last celt. It was used as an adze[1].

The Bylany archaeologists processed more than 70,000 sherds. They used punched cards to help them, and it was one of the first uses of computing in archaeology[3]. It is the decoration of the sherds that makes it possible to put the houses in order in time.

One thing is missing at Bylany. Despite great efforts, no cemetery of this culture has been found there[3].

So we have to look for the graves of the first farmers elsewhere, in southern Moravia.

4. Vedrovice: What the Graves Reveal

At Vedrovice near Znojmo, the Moravian Museum excavated from 1961 a settlement enclosed by a ditch and two cemeteries of the Linear Pottery culture[11]. In one of them the archaeologists uncovered 96 graves from the early phase of this culture[7]. Radiocarbon dates place them at roughly 5300 to 5200 BC, shortly before the well at Ostrov was built[21].

The bodies of the dead were sprinkled with a red pigment, ochre. They were given vessels, stone tools and ornaments of marble, of animal teeth and above all of the shells of a sea bivalve of the genus Spondylus[7]. In grave number 46 lay a young man. He was 166 centimetres tall, an ordinary height at the time. With him were a small vessel, a stone axe, chipped tools and a large Spondylus pendant[7].

The natural sciences revealed most about these people. Biochemical and genetic analyses of the skeletons showed that they were surprisingly mobile[7]. Using strontium isotopes, researchers can tell who was local and who came from elsewhere[21]. According to the researchers, some moved into the village only during their lives. Others grew up here, left and came back years later[7]. So people, like the sea shells, travelled across Europe.

Another view into their world is offered by wells like the one from Ostrov. In the wet, things survived in them that rot elsewhere: wood, bark and bast. Thanks to them we know that people drew water in small containers of birch bark too, and that they could repair a cracked clay vessel with pitch[5]. Wells from the beginning of the Neolithic are now known from more than fifty sites in Europe[20].

Around 5000 BC the Linear Pottery culture comes to an end[1]. In Bohemia it is followed by the Stroked Pottery culture: the decoration of vessels now consists of rows of punctures instead of incised lines[1].

And it is in its time that structures appear in Central Europe unlike anything that had been here before.

5. Rondels

We call them rondels. A rondel is a circular enclosure: one to four ditches with a pointed bottom and, inside, one to three circular trenches in which the posts of a palisade stood. Two to six entrances led in, most often four[6]. The diameter is usually 30 to 80 metres, exceptionally more than a hundred[6]. We know about two hundred of them in Europe, from Bavaria and Saxony-Anhalt through Poland, Czechia and Austria to south-western Slovakia and western Hungary[6][9].

Their dating rests on radiocarbon. Objects from the ditches fall at roughly 4850 to 4600 BC, perhaps only until 4700[9]. So rondels are more than 1,500 years older than Stonehenge, where the first ditch and bank were made around 3000 BC[9][22]. And they were built only for a short time, a hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty years[9].

The first rondel in Central Europe that archaeologists investigated in full lies near Těšetice-Kyjovice in the Znojmo region. It belongs to the Moravian Painted Ware culture. Its ditch is interrupted by four gates facing the cardinal points, and a palisade stood both inside and outside the ditch[10].

In 2022 the Institute of Archaeology of the Czech Academy of Sciences excavated the rondel at Prague-Vinoř. It is about 55 metres across and has an unusual three entrances. Houses were about to be built on the field, so the archaeologists expected to uncover about nine tenths of the ground plan[9].

Two rondels also stood at Bylany. After a gap of about three hundred years, people with Stroked Pottery came back there[3].

What were rondels for? Here the finds end and the hypotheses begin. The older interpretation saw them as a fortress or a cattle pen. Several entrances and a bank on the outer side of the ditch speak against that. Other researchers think of a sanctuary or a place for observing the movement of heavenly bodies[6]. Archaeologists from the Prague Institute of Archaeology consider it most likely that rondels served mainly as gathering places for people from a wide area[6]. The objects from the ditches do not help. They most likely got there only after the rondel had gone out of use[6].

What exactly went on inside, though, we do not know. The only certain thing is that many people from different villages had to come together to build one[6].

The rondels disappeared, and around the middle of the 5th millennium a period begins that brought the first metal.

6. Copper, the Plough and the Wagon

We call that period the Eneolithic, the late Stone Age. In Bohemia it begins around 4500 to 4400 BC and ends around 2300 to 2200 BC, when the Bronze Age sets in[2]. So it lasts more than two thousand years.

It is sometimes called the Copper Age[2]. The first copper objects in Bohemia, however, are small: beads of sheet metal and wire rings from graves at Bílina and at Prague-Bubeneč. Only later do heavy objects appear, axes and axe-hammers[2]. Copper metallurgy developed first in the Balkans, around the middle of the 5th millennium; later copper was also worked in the northern foothills of the Alps[2]. That it was worked in our lands too is shown by a crucible with traces of copper from the settlement at Makotřasy near Prague[2]. The settlement belongs to the Funnel Beaker culture, which archaeologists place from about 3800 BC onwards[2]. It was surrounded by an unusual quadrangular enclosure. The excavator interprets it as a monument shaped by the astronomical knowledge of the people of the time. That, too, is a hypothesis[2].

But archaeologists do not regard copper as the most striking economic change of the Eneolithic. It is the ard, the simple plough[2]. Cattle ceased to be only a source of meat and milk and began to pull[2]. As late as 2008, researchers cited a single direct piece of evidence of ploughing from Bohemia: traces under a long barrow at Březno near Louny[2].

And the wagon? That cattle also pulled wagons we know mainly from neighbouring countries. From Poland come engravings and clay models of wagons, from bogs in the Netherlands and northern Germany solid wooden wheels. In Moravia, at Holubice, skulls of cattle deformed by traction were found[2].

Settlements change too. The large Neolithic villages disappear and are replaced by smaller ones[2]. In the middle Eneolithic, settlements grow up on hilltops, surrounded by ditches. The best investigated is Homolka near Stehelčeves, a settlement of the Řivnáč culture. Archaeologists identified 25 to 39 ground plans of dwellings there, which certainly did not all stand at the same time[2].

People moved in the Eneolithic as well. A genetic study of skeletons from Bohemia showed that people of the Funnel Beaker culture were for the most part of non-local origin[16].

But the greatest change came only at the beginning of the 3rd millennium.

7. Corded Ware and Ancestors from the Steppe

Around 2900 BC, a century later at the latest, the Corded Ware culture appears in Bohemia[2][16]. It is named after decoration made by impressions of cord[2]. We know it almost only from graves[2]. About 1,500 of them are recorded in Bohemia[16].

And those graves follow a strict order. Men lie on their right side with the head to the west, women on their left side with the head to the east, and all of them face south. Men usually have a stone axe-hammer in the grave, women a necklace[2].

Where did these people come from? Archaeologists argued about it throughout the 20th century. As late as 2008, a summary work on the Bohemian Eneolithic stated that the origin of the culture could not be clearly determined and that its character seemed Central European[2].

Then genetics spoke. A study from 2015 found that Corded Ware people from Germany shared about three quarters of their ancestry with herders of the Yamnaya culture from the steppes of eastern Europe. The authors called it evidence of a massive migration[17].

A more detailed picture came from a study of 271 prehistoric genomes from Bohemia itself[16]. It confirmed the steppe ancestry but added important reservations. The earliest Corded Ware people were genetically very diverse. At the cemetery of Vliněves in central Bohemia, three of the fifteen early individuals examined had no steppe ancestry at all. Yet their graves did not differ from the others. Among the early individuals without steppe ancestry the study found only women, four in all[16]. And according to the authors, the steppe ancestry cannot be derived entirely from the Yamnaya skeletons known so far. Where exactly the first Corded Ware people came from remains open[16].

So genetics says that new people came. It does not say how many there were, or whether they came in peace. According to the authors, identical graves for people of different origins show that newcomers and locals joined into one society[16].

After a few centuries the burial customs changed again and were turned literally inside out.

8. The Bell Beakers

Around 2500 BC the Bell Beaker culture appears in Bohemia[2][16]. The richly decorated beakers it is named after spread at that time across a large part of western and central Europe[18].

The rules of burial are a mirror image. Men now lie on their left side with the head to the north, women on their right side with the head to the south, and all of them face east[2]. Men were given an archer's kit for the grave: a stone wrist-guard, arrows with flint heads and often a copper dagger as well. Rich women's graves contain ornaments of sheet gold and amber buttons[2].

Yet hunting did not play a large part in these people's economy. At the settlement of Liptice near Teplice, the bones of wild animals make up barely a tenth, and elsewhere even less. So archery was probably connected more with the symbolism of combat than with hunting[2].

Did people spread with the beakers, or only ideas? A large genetic study covered more than two hundred individuals buried with objects of this culture. Its answer: both, depending on where. People from the Iberian Peninsula and from Central Europe were genetically far apart. So between these regions it was mainly ideas that spread. Most of the people examined outside Iberia, however, had a large share of steppe ancestry. In Britain, by contrast, new people arrived with the beakers, and within a few centuries about nine tenths of the gene pool there was replaced[18].

One grave of this culture takes us back to a familiar place. In 2010, near Těšetice-Kyjovice, at the site with the rondel, the chamber grave of a man of high standing was discovered[10].

The oldest radiocarbon dates of the Únětice culture, which comes next in Bohemia, lie around 2300 BC[2]. With that the Stone Age ends.

Before we leave it, let us sum up what to take from those three millennia.

9. What to Remember

First: farming did not arise in our lands. It came from the Near East and arrived here with the Linear Pottery culture in the middle of the 6th millennium BC. According to DNA from prehistoric bones, it was brought mainly by new people[1][16][17][19].

Second: these people built longhouses and knew how to work wood. The well from Ostrov, from the turn of 5256 and 5255 BC, is proof of that[3][12][20].

Third: rondels were built only for a short time, roughly between 4850 and 4600 BC. What they were for, we do not know[6][9].

Fourth: the Eneolithic brought copper, the plough and the wagon[2].

And fifth: around 2900 BC, a century later at the latest, people with steppe ancestry arrived with the Corded Ware culture. They were followed by the Bell Beaker culture[2][16].

Two questions remain open. What were rondels for? And where exactly did the first Corded Ware people come from? Perhaps only further excavations and further DNA analyses will answer both.

Next time the Bronze Age awaits us.
