1. The empty tomb

In December 1976, an expert commission in St Vitus Cathedral at Prague Castle opened the tomb of King Přemysl Ottokar II. The stone tomb, carved for him almost a hundred years later by the workshop of Peter Parler, was empty inside. Only beneath the floor did they find a small chamber, and in it a plain oak coffin.[11]

Inside lay bones, a bundle of rare half-silk fabrics and funeral insignia. On the skull was a crown cut from gilded silver sheet, and beside it a sceptre and an orb. And between the jaws lay a lead tablet with an inscription.[11][12]

The skull also bore a wound. According to the anthropologist Emanuel Vlček, it was made by a cutting weapon, most likely a battle axe. The blow, he believed, came from above, while the king was lying down or kneeling. But that is his interpretation, and he first had to piece the skull together from fragments.[11][12]

This king died in a battle that he lost. Yet only a few years earlier he had ruled from the Giant Mountains all the way to the sea. A contemporary in Prague wrote that he had been called “from his mother's womb” to be a golden king. And a monk in Erfurt, in Germany, writing about thirteen years after the king's death, recorded that even the Tatars called him the iron king.[27] Hence the Iron and Golden King.[1][27]

Even among the Tatars he was called the iron king.
Chronicle of St Peter's Abbey in Erfurt, year 1278, our translation from the Latin

This episode is about the last Přemyslids on the Bohemian throne. We begin in 1253. We will see a realm that reached the Adriatic, and the battle in which it collapsed. Then come the evil years of famine, the powerful Záviš, silver from Kutná Hora and a new coin. And we end with a knife in Olomouc in 1306.[1][2]

2. Heir of the Babenbergs and crusader

Přemysl Ottokar was born in 1232, possibly 1233. He became King of Bohemia after his father in 1253. But by then he was already ruling Austria as well. The Austrian lords had chosen him as their lord, and he had married Margaret, of the extinct Babenberg dynasty, the widow of a King of the Romans. She was considerably older than he was.[2][4]

Austria was rich. An annalist wrote that in 1254 the king came back with a chest full of gifts. The clerics at court called it Noah's Ark. Yet in the same year, Bohemia suffered a failed harvest and a dearth “such as our age does not remember”.[1]

In December, Ottokar marched north, “girded with the sign of the cross”, against the pagan Prussians. It was a crusade. According to the annalist, the army plundered, sparing “neither sex nor age”.[1]

The Prussian chiefs submitted and were baptised. Ottokar stood godfather to the most powerful of them and gave him his own name. The godfathers then gave their own clothes to the newly baptised. Then the crusaders came to a hill, named it the king's mountain, and built a fort on it. According to historians, this expedition gave rise to Königsberg, named after the Bohemian king, and known today as Kaliningrad.[1][4]

Back home, meanwhile, the king was rebuilding Prague. In 1257, according to the annalist, he “drove the Czechs out from below the castle and settled foreigners there”. A new town grew up beneath the castle, today's Malá Strana, the Lesser Town, and it was given stone walls.[1][18]

Archaeologists uncovered a section of that wall in Thunovská Street in 2014. It was almost two metres thick and may have survived to a height of 8 metres. The wall itself was kept, but it disappeared behind the walls of the rebuilt house. The stone-lined ditch in front of it, however, the authorities allowed to be demolished. According to the archaeologists, such a permit is exceptional in Central Europe.[18]

3. A river like the Red Sea

The Hungarian king, Béla IV, also wanted Austria and Styria. The dispute came to a head in the summer of 1260 at Kressenbrunn, on the Marchfeld, the Moravian Field, not far from Vienna.[1][4]

We know about the battle mainly from a letter that Ottokar himself sent to the Pope. So it is the victor's account. The two camps were separated by the river Morava, the March in German. Among his enemies, the king lists Cumans, Hungarians, Székelys, Vlachs, Bulgarians and even “heretic Bosnians”.[1]

Both sides agreed to a truce. But at night, the Hungarians secretly crossed the fords. According to the king, “barely a tenth part” of his army remained in the Bohemian camp; the rest had gone off elsewhere. The enemy surrounded them in a half-circle.[1]

And yet Ottokar's army won. For those who fled, the king wrote, the river Morava was “like the Red Sea to the Egyptians”, just as the sea in the Bible swallowed Pharaoh's army. Some, he says, got across the river “over the bodies of men and horses, as if over bridges”.[1]

The river Morava was to them like the Red Sea to the Egyptians.
Letter of Ottokar II to the Pope, 1260, our translation from V. V. Tomek's Czech

The battle was fought on 12, possibly 13 July. Béla then gave up Styria. The Prague chronicler added a miracle to the battle. A knight supposedly saw in a vision Saints Wenceslas, Adalbert and Procopius hurrying to help the Bohemian army. It is a pious story, not a report from the battlefield.[1][4]

Then the king's life changed. In October 1261, Queen Margaret left for Austria, “for what reasons, God knows”, wrote the annalist. The Zbraslav Chronicle explains. They had no children, so the king divorced her with the Pope's consent.[1][2][4]

That same month, Ottokar married Kunigunde, the granddaughter of his defeated rival Béla. At Christmas, the Archbishop of Mainz crowned them both in Prague. The feast lasted two days on the Letná plain, in buildings put up just for the celebration.[1]

4. The golden king

In 1264, a wedding took place near Pressburg, today's Bratislava. Ottokar's niece was marrying the son of the Hungarian king. For the occasion, Ottokar had a bridge built across the Danube on large boats. The Hungarian king arrived with golden chains hanging at his temples. And, following Hungarian custom, a nobleman lifted the crown from the bride's head with a drawn sword.[1]

It is at this wedding that a contemporary Prague cleric writes of Ottokar that “from his mother's womb he was called a golden king”. And in a eulogy after his death, he writes that among the kings from the rising of the sun to its setting, there was no one so generous.[1]

…who from his mother's womb was called a golden king.
History of King Přemysl Ottokar II, our translation from the Latin

In 1269, Ottokar also gained Carinthia and Carniola, lands in today's Austria and Slovenia. The Zbraslav Chronicle says that he “spread his branches as far as the sea”. According to a German biography, for about a quarter of a century he ruled the largest continuous dominion in Central Europe.[2][4]

And he founded towns. According to the same biography, he founded or raised the status of more than fifty. They include Písek, Polička, Uherské Hradiště and Marchegg in Austria. He invited foreigners into the towns, often German-speaking craftsmen and settlers, and granted them privileges. He confirmed the rights of the Jews in a special charter.[4][25]

In the south of Bohemia, around 1265, the burgrave Hirzo laid out a new town where the Malše flows into the Vltava. České Budějovice was given a square measuring 133 by 137 metres. In the courtyard of today's town hall, archaeologists found its oldest traces: postholes, rubbish pits and three small cellars.[8][19]

Also in the south of Bohemia, in 1263, the king founded the Cistercian monastery of Zlatá Koruna, the Golden Crown. But the nobles founded monasteries too. The powerful Vítkovci clan had had its own monastery at Vyšší Brod since 1259.[2][4][9][10]

The king also thought about everyday matters. In 1268, he had wolf pits dug in every village. A goose or a piglet served as bait. And he ordered weights and measures to be renewed and marked with his coat of arms.[1]

5. Five banners

Ottokar was named several times among the candidates for the crown of the King of the Romans, the elected head of the Holy Roman Empire. The History of King Přemysl Ottokar even claims that in 1271 the prince-electors offered it to him and he refused. Whether that is true, we do not know for certain.[1][4]

In 1273, the electors chose Rudolf of Habsburg, without the Bohemian king. Ottokar did not recognise him and did not ask him to confirm his lands. There followed the imperial ban, then excommunication, and finally war.[4]

The chronicler put a proud reply into Ottokar's mouth. Rudolf, he says, had never held the lands he was demanding. Some Ottokar had received as a dowry, and some he had “won with a victorious hand and the sword”.[1]

And some I have won with a victorious hand and the sword.
History of King Přemysl Ottokar II, our translation from V. V. Tomek's Czech

But in 1276, a blow came from within. According to the annalist, the Vítkovci “with great military force fell away from the king”, just when he needed them most. Other nobles rose against the king as well. Meanwhile, the Bohemian army went hungry for seven weeks near Drosendorf in Austria.[1][4]

In November 1276, Ottokar gave up Austria, Styria, Carinthia, Carniola and Cheb. As a sign of submission, he handed Rudolf five banners. Rudolf gave two of them back, for Bohemia and Moravia. He granted him those lands as fiefs.[1][4]

The peace was to be sealed by a double marriage. Ottokar's son Wenceslas was to marry Rudolf's daughter Guta, and Rudolf's son was to marry Ottokar's daughter. But the calm did not last. Ottokar was preparing to strike back. The annalist also recorded an evil omen. A huge number of wolves supposedly gathered in front of Prague Castle and howled.[1][4]

6. The Marchfeld

On 27 June 1278, the king marched into Moravia. Against him stood Rudolf, and with Rudolf the King of Hungary and his Cumans.[1]

The decisive battle came on 26 August 1278, between the villages of Dürnkrut and Jedenspeigen on the Marchfeld. It was not far from where Ottokar had won eighteen years earlier. A German biography counts it among the greatest knightly battles in Europe. It was decided by a charge of heavy cavalry that Rudolf had held in reserve.[4]

The Austrian Rhymed Chronicle, written twenty or more years later, describes it vividly. The reserve, it says, cut through the ranks “as a tailor cuts cloth with his shears”. Ottokar sent a lord named Milota to bring up his own reserve. But Milota remembered that the king had burned his brother, and rode away with it. The other sources we have read say nothing about this betrayal.[3]

The Zbraslav Chronicle sees it differently. Many Bohemians, it says, fled “like thieves” and abandoned their king, who was fighting bravely in the midst of his enemies.[2]

And how did the king die? The contemporary Prague author honestly admits that he knows nothing for certain. The king is said to have vanished between the armies and was never seen again. The Zbraslav Chronicle writes that he was captured and quickly killed.[1][2]

Of the death of King Ottokar we can tell nothing certain.
History of King Přemysl Ottokar II, our translation from V. V. Tomek's Czech

The Rhymed Chronicle, on the other hand, knows far too much. The king, it says, was knocked from his horse and begged for his life. The knight Siegfried of Merenberg answered him: “You will pay me for my uncle.” And he stabbed him. Then servants stripped the dead king, and he lay naked on the ground. He was covered with the cloak of another man's servant.[3]

The Italian contemporary Thomas of Tuscany wrote that the body was recognised by its clothes. One blow had knocked out an eye, another had split the skull. That fits well with the wound the anthropologist saw on the skull seven hundred years later.[12]

The body was embalmed and put on display in Vienna. There were no royal honours, because the king was under the ban. Then he was taken to the Franciscan friars in Znojmo, in southern Moravia. The body returned to Prague only in his son's time, in 1296 to 1297.[11][2]

O wheel of Fortune, a wheel as fickle as the wheel of the moon.
Zbraslav Chronicle, our translation from the Latin

7. The evil years

Ottokar's son Wenceslas was not yet even seven years old. Rudolf placed Bohemia for five years under the guardianship of Margrave Otto of Brandenburg. Moravia he kept for himself.[2][5]

Otto had Queen Kunigunde and little Wenceslas loaded onto a cart at night, while they slept, and taken to Bezděz Castle in northern Bohemia. They were guarded by Germans, and barely three Czechs were allowed in to them. The queen later begged to be allowed to travel to her husband's funeral, and escaped. The Prague author remarked that “sometimes lies are useful”.[1]

Wenceslas was taken away to Brandenburg. The Zbraslav Chronicle tells how he went hungry there and walked about in torn shoes. He supposedly did not have even a penny to get them mended.[2]

Meanwhile in Bohemia, Otto's men went looking for treasure. In January 1279, they looted the treasury of Prague's cathedral church and even searched the tomb of Saint Wenceslas.[1]

O money, queen of all evil!
Account of the Evil Years, our translation from V. V. Tomek's Czech

The country fought back. At Christmas 1280, an assembly decided that the foreign Germans had to leave within three days. According to the chronicler, “they vanished like smoke”. And “the farmhand began to work with his plough, the smith in his smithy”.[1]

But then came famine. A contemporary wrote that a penny could buy barely two eggs. Yet he remembered times when it bought fifty. He called the year 1282 the year of the great famine. Craftsmen who had once had property were begging.[1]

The Prague councillors hired gravediggers. According to the author, they dug eight pits, and each held about a thousand bodies. They were full within six months. And Prague, in his words, was “not even a thirtieth part” of the kingdom.[1]

Otto made sure he was paid well for releasing Wenceslas, in the end with another twenty thousand marks of silver and castles pledged as security. On 24 May 1283, the eleven-year-old king returned to Prague. People greeted him with the hymn “Lord, have mercy on us”.[1][2]

8. Záviš

Meanwhile, Queen Kunigunde had grown close to Záviš of Falkenstein. He came from the Vítkovci clan, and Ottokar had once driven him out of the country. The Zbraslav Chronicle puts his success with the queen down to “magical deceptions”. But the chronicler hated Záviš.[2]

They had a son, Jan. When Wenceslas returned, Záviš became the most powerful man in the land. “When the king is silent, he speaks,” the chronicler wrote of him. He married Kunigunde in 1284, possibly 1285. In September 1285, the queen died.[2][5]

At Vyšší Brod, the family monastery of the Vítkovci, a golden double-armed cross known as the Záviš Cross is still kept today. It weighs over four kilos, and under rock crystal it holds a relic of the cross of Christ. The monastery probably received it in the 1280s. Where it came from is disputed among experts. Hungary is mentioned most often, but it is not certain.[16]

The chronicler claims that Záviš wanted to kill the king. That is an accusation from a hostile author. Zbraslav was Wenceslas's own foundation.[2]

Around the turn of 1288 and 1289, Wenceslas invited Záviš to Prague and had him seized at the castle. The army then marched with him to Hluboká. The defenders would not surrender the castle. And so Záviš was beheaded below it, before the eyes of his brothers. According to a record from Vyšší Brod, this happened on 24 August 1290. He was buried at Vyšší Brod.[2][5][10][16]

9. The silver king

Wenceslas II was not like his father. According to the Zbraslav chronicler, he was afraid of thunderstorms. He had a hollow altar made and hid inside it during storms, while a learned man read to him from Scripture at the entrance. And he hated cats; he could not even bear the sight of them.[2]

He wanted to rule by written law. He brought in an expert in law from Italy, Gozzo of Orvieto, but the nobles stood against it. The university he wanted to found in Prague met the same fate.[2]

In 1292, he founded a monastery at Zbraslav and named it Aula Regia, the Royal Court. In 1297, he had himself crowned. According to the chronicler, the guests received fodder for 191,000 horses. And wine flowed from the fountains in the New Market. That number is more likely an exaggeration.[2]

He could afford all of it. Because he had Kutná Hora. A charter from 1289 settles a dispute between two towns over a new mining hill. Legend says the silver was found by a monk from Sedlec, who left his cowl, his kutna, on the spot. The Zbraslav chronicler called the place “the tinder of greed and the abyss of wickedness”.[2][6][23]

Kutná Hora, tinder of greed and abyss of wickedness.
Zbraslav Chronicle, our translation from the Latin

Under today's Jesuit College, archaeologists uncovered shafts, channels for water, probably also places where the ore was washed, and ruts that carts had worn into the bedrock. Mining went on there from the end of the thirteenth century.[21]

And then came the reform. “In the year of the Lord 1300, in the month of July,” the chronicle says, “the coinage of Prague groschen and small pennies was introduced.” Twelve small pennies made one groschen. The reform was prepared by three men from Florence.[2][13]

On the obverse of the groschen are the Bohemian crown and the name of King Wenceslas II; on the reverse, the Bohemian lion and the inscription GROSSI PRAGENSES. The model was the French groat of Tours. And for almost a quarter of a millennium, the Prague groschen shaped trade in Central Europe. The chronicler lamented that many people regard the groschen “almost as a god”.[2][7][13][14]

Silver also came with a new mining code, the Ius regale montanorum. It was drawn up between 1300 and 1305, and it banned selling or exporting silver freely. Whoever mined it had to exchange it for groschen.[6]

10. Three crowns and a knife in Olomouc

Silver also brought crowns. In the summer of 1300, Wenceslas was crowned King of Poland in Gniezno. According to the chronicler, Poles and Czechs “do not differ much in the Slavic tongue”. A year later, his son, also named Wenceslas, received the Hungarian crown. He ruled Hungary under the name Ladislaus.[2][5]

But a neighbour grew afraid. The King of the Romans, Albert of Habsburg, demanded Kutná Hora. When he did not get it, he marched on the town in the autumn of 1304. According to the chronicle, the miners let silver slag into the stream that his camp drank from. The army then withdrew, worn down by hunger, disease and cold.[2]

They thirsted for silver; they drank silver slag.
Zbraslav Chronicle, our translation from the Latin

Wenceslas II died on 21 June 1305, after a long illness. He was thirty-three years old. The castle had burned down, so he died in the house of the goldsmith Konrad. This was most likely the same Konrad whom the sources list among the first minters of the groschen.[2][13]

His son was still a young man. He was a “king of three kingdoms” and spoke four languages: Latin, German, Hungarian and Czech. But the chronicler complains that he drank with the nobles and, when drunk, handed out charters to them. With a pun, he called them “privilegia, or rather pravilegia”. The Latin word pravus means crooked, so in his eyes they were not privileges but rather crooked charters. Because of them, the king was still losing estates in the chronicler's own day.[2]

These privilegia, or rather pravilegia, still deprive the king of many estates to this day.
Zbraslav Chronicle, our translation from the Latin

Wenceslas III gave up Hungary, and in the summer of 1306 he set out for Poland. On the way, he stopped in Olomouc, in Moravia. He was staying in the dean's house at the castle. On 4 August, to cool off in the summer heat, he was alone in the palace, wearing only a shirt and a cloak. There he received three fatal wounds. He was sixteen years old.[2][24]

A knight, Konrad of Botenstein, supposedly ran out of the palace with a bloody knife. He was seized at once and killed before he could speak. The Zbraslav chronicler admitted: “Whether he or another is guilty, I do not know; God knows.”[2]

Wenceslas III was the last male of the Přemyslid dynasty. His body was later taken to Zbraslav, where the Hussites smashed the royal tombs in 1420. A skull that was long believed there to be his most likely does not belong to him, according to a more recent interpretation.[2][17][24]

So who killed the last Přemyslid? And who was behind it? We still do not know. The Bohemian crown was left without a king. In the next episode, we will see who takes it, and how this leads to Charles IV.
