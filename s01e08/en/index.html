1. Brothers at the church door

A duke walks to church in the morning. On the way he meets his brother, embraces him and thanks him for the feast the day before. The brother draws a sword from under his cloak. “Yesterday, as the moment allowed. But now this is how a brother serves a brother.” That is how Christian's Legend describes Wenceslas's last morning. The duke survives the first blow and catches the bare blade with his hand. But at the church door, more armed men catch up with him.[9]

Yesterday, as the moment allowed. But now this is how a brother serves a brother.
Christian's Legend, ch. 7; our translation from the Czech of A. L. Stříž

The two brothers are Wenceslas and Boleslaus. One will become the patron saint of the land. The other will turn it into a Central European power.[9][25]

We will follow their story in four steps. First Bořivoj, the first Christian duke, and his strongholds. Then Ludmila, Drahomíra and the young Wenceslas. Then Boleslaus, his wars and his realm. And finally Prague full of foreign merchants, the first bishop, and a bloody day at Libice.[1][5][7][9][11][13][17]

Right at the start we run into something odd. We know the day Wenceslas died: 28 September. But not the year. The chronicler Cosmas wrote 929. Today's historians consider 935 more likely. We will explain why.[4][9][10][22]

Three kinds of witness tell us about the tenth century: the finds of archaeologists, the records of chroniclers and the legends of saints. The legends are the most colourful, but they were written to glorify a saint. So we will always say who is speaking.[1][8]

2. Bořivoj between Moravia and Bohemia

Bořivoj is the first Přemyslid mentioned in historical records. Before him, the family legend places a line of mythical ancestors. At their head stands Přemysl the ploughman, whom the people chose as their duke on the advice of a prophetess. Christian already tells it this way. It is a legend about the origin of the dynasty, not a documented list of rulers.[8][13]

With Bořivoj comes a story that is easy to remember. The duke travelled to Moravia to visit Svatopluk, the ruler of Great Moravia, and was invited to a feast. But as a pagan he was not allowed to sit at the table among Christians. They seated him on the floor.[8]

Bishop Methodius reportedly asked him whether such a powerful man was not ashamed to sit on the ground with herdsmen for the sake of pagan idols. And he made him a promise: renounce your idols, and you will become lord of your lords. Bořivoj replied: “Then why hesitate to be baptised?”[9]

This scene is a legend, and nobody wrote down the exact words spoken at that table. But one thing we can take from it with confidence. At that time, baptism meant more than faith. It was entry into the circle of rulers who dealt with each other as equals. And historians accept as fact that Bořivoj was baptised from Moravia at a time when Svatopluk also controlled Bohemia. It most likely happened in the 880s.[1][8][13]

After his return, Bořivoj built the church of St Clement. It is considered the oldest church in Bohemia, and it stood at Levý Hradec, on the left bank of the Vltava, not far from Prague.[2][8]

Archaeologists found a hillfort there, settled from roughly the middle of the 9th century. It was protected by a rampart of timber and earth, faced on the outside with a stone wall. But Bořivoj's church itself has not yet been found. The rotunda whose foundations lie under today's church is later, Romanesque work.[1][2]

Bořivoj had his second church built in Prague itself. The church of the Virgin Mary stood on the Prague Castle promontory from the end of the 9th century. Archaeologists uncovered its walls in 1950. A grave found inside is attributed to Bořivoj's son Spytihněv and his wife.[3][14]

Then everything changed. In 894, Svatopluk died. The Bohemian leaders reacted quickly.[6]

In July 895, they came to King Arnulf in Regensburg, in Bavaria. The Annals of Fulda name the two foremost among them: Spytihněv and Vitislav. By placing their hands in the king's, they submitted to him. With that, Bohemia broke away from Moravia and turned to Bavaria.[6]

Notice that there are two dukes, and others came with them. Bohemia was not yet a single state. The Přemyslids controlled only its centre.[1][6][25]

It was Spytihněv who consolidated this centre. He ruled until 915, and his power rested on strongholds around Prague. They included Budeč, Tetín and Boleslav. Each of them later became the seat of a member of the ducal family.[25]

Spytihněv was succeeded by his brother Vratislav. He ruled only briefly, until 921. At Prague Castle he left behind the church of St George. And at home, two little sons, Wenceslas and Boleslaus.[9][14][25]

Who would rule on their behalf? Two women now clashed over that.[9][13]

3. Ludmila and Drahomíra

The first was Ludmila, Bořivoj's widow and the grandmother of both boys. The second was their mother Drahomíra. She came from a land of pagan Slavs called Stodor.[9][13]

Christian tells us that little Wenceslas was given to his grandmother to be brought up. Drahomíra began to fear that, with the boy in Ludmila's hands, she would lose power too. Ludmila gave way. She left Prague for the stronghold of Tetín, above the Berounka river southwest of Prague.[9]

It did not help. Drahomíra sent two of her nobles after her with an armed band. The legend even knows their names: Tunna and Gommon. In the evening they broke down the door and strangled Ludmila.[9]

It happened in 921. Christian is writing the legend of a saint, so in his telling Ludmila is holy and Drahomíra is wicked. But behind the story we can see an ordinary political dispute. Whoever raises the future duke holds power.[9][13]

You can visit Tetín today. A hillfort really did stand there; it was built at the turn of the ninth and tenth centuries. But the castle ruin and the churches you will see there are younger. What remains from Ludmila's time is mainly the promontory and the view over the river.[16]

Meanwhile, according to the legend, Wenceslas studied at Budeč, northwest of Prague. Christian says his father sent him there to learn his letters.[9][15]

And at Budeč stands something exceptional. The rotunda of St Peter and St Paul. According to Christian, it was founded by Duke Spytihněv. It still stands today, and it is the oldest standing stone building in Bohemia. When you step inside, you are among walls that remember the first Přemyslids.[9][15]

Wenceslas came of age, took over the government and for a time drove his mother out of the country. He had Ludmila's body moved from Tetín to Prague, to the church of St George. But soon a bigger worry than family quarrels awaited him.[9]

4. Wenceslas between faith and power

The legend portrays Wenceslas as a duke who grinds the flour himself, bakes the hosts and presses the wine for Mass. Christian adds one more detail. When there were not enough children for the Easter baptism, Wenceslas reportedly sent to the market, bought all the boys who were there for sale and had them baptised.[9]

The legend wants to show his piety. But it lets slip something else: at the Prague market, people were routinely sold. We will come back to that.[9][25]

But Wenceslas was no monk. He was a duke with a retinue and with powerful neighbours. In 929, the Saxon king Henry marched on Prague with an army. The chronicler Widukind writes that the duke of Prague submitted to him, and that the king made Bohemia pay tribute.[7][12]

The Saxon chronicler presents it as a complete victory for his king. Today's historians are more cautious. What is certain is that from then on, Bohemia paid tribute to the empire.[7][12][22]

At Prague Castle, Wenceslas was building a new church at the time, the rotunda of St Vitus. He had to ask the bishop of Regensburg for permission to build it. Today the cathedral stands in its place.[3][9][14]

Then came an invitation from his brother. Boleslaus lived at his own stronghold, in today's Stará Boleslav, northeast of Prague. He invited Wenceslas to the feast of Saints Cosmas and Damian, and to a banquet. What happened the next morning, we already know.[9][22]

It is not only the Czech legends that say Boleslaus killed his brother. Widukind writes it too, in Saxony: Boleslaus, he says, killed his brother, a Christian most devoted to God. Two independent voices agree here.[7][9]

Why did he do it? The legend speaks of lust for power and of the devil's whispering. Historians see a power struggle between brothers who also disagreed about policy towards their neighbours. Once in power, Boleslaus stopped paying the empire and went to war with the Saxons.[7][9][22]

And now the promised explanation of the two dates. Cosmas wrote that Wenceslas died in 929. But Cosmas was writing two hundred years later. Widukind, who was a contemporary, links the murder to the time when a new king, Otto, came to the throne in Saxony. And Otto came to the throne in 936. That is why most historians today prefer the year 935.[4][7][10][22]

Three years later, Boleslaus had Wenceslas's body moved to Prague, to the rotunda of St Vitus. The dead duke soon became a saint. And his murderer ruled Bohemia for several more decades.[9][22]

In history books, Boleslaus bears the epithet the Cruel. Older historians set him against Wenceslas: the weak saint against the strong ruler. Today's historians do not see it in such stark terms. Both brothers built the same ducal power, each by different means.[12][22]

5. Boleslaus and his realm

Boleslaus's war with the Saxons began with a victory. He attacked a neighbouring prince who was relying on Saxon help. The Saxons sent two armies. Boleslaus divided his forces against them. He put one army to flight. The other he caught off guard while it was gathering booty after winning a skirmish, and destroyed it along with its commander. Then he took his neighbour's stronghold.[7]

King Otto did not let it rest. Widukind writes that the war lasted until the fourteenth year of the king's reign. It was not one great battle. It was years of raids and retaliatory campaigns across the border mountains.[7]

The decision came in 950. Otto invaded Bohemia with a strong army and besieged a stronghold the chronicler calls Nova, meaning “New”. It was defended by Boleslaus's son. Where that stronghold stood is still debated by historians.[7]

The decisive assault never came. Seeing the strength of the royal army, Boleslaus chose to submit instead. He stood beneath the banners, listened to the king and was pardoned. He remained duke, recognised Otto's overlordship, and the enemy became an ally.[7][22]

That became clear five years later. On 10 August 955, by the river Lech in Bavaria, Otto's army met the Hungarians, whose mounted raids were ravaging Europe. Otto's army included a thousand warriors from Bohemia.[22][25]

Most of them fell in the battle. But Otto won, and the age of the great Hungarian raids came to an end.[25]

For Boleslaus, this opened the way east. Within a few years, he took control of northern Moravia, Silesia and the lands as far as Kraków, in today's southern Poland. A traveller we will hear more about in a moment called him the king of Prague, Bohemia and Kraków. It reportedly took three weeks to travel from one end of his land to the other.[18][22][25]

Boleslaus, king of Prague, Bohemia and Kraków
Ibrahim ibn Yaqub, account of the Slavs, 960s; our translation

It was not a unified state. It was a set of territories that the duke held by force and from which he took plunder. And the most valuable plunder was people. Captives from pagan lands ended up as slaves at the Prague market. According to the historian Jiří Sláma, this was Boleslaus's largest source of income.[25]

Besides the sword, Boleslaus also used marriage. In 965, his daughter Doubravka married the Polish duke Mieszko. Two years later, Boleslaus sent his warriors to help his son-in-law.[12][22]

And where did the merchants' caravans from this whole great realm head? To Prague.[18][25]

6. Prague, silver and trade

In the 960s, a man named Ibrahim ibn Yaqub came to Prague. He was an educated Jew from Arab Spain, travelling through Europe in the service of the caliph of Córdoba. He wrote an account of his journey, and thanks to it we know how Prague looked to foreign eyes at that time.[18][25]

And it is an admiring description. Ibrahim wrote: “The city of Prague is built of stone and lime, and it is the richest of the lands in goods.”[18]

The city of Prague is built of stone and lime, and it is the richest of the lands in goods.
Ibrahim ibn Yaqub, account of the Slavs, 960s; our translation

Who traded there? According to him, Rus and Slavs came from Kraków with goods. Those Rus were most likely Varangians from Kyiv. From the Hungarian lands and from the lands of Islam came Jews and Hungarians. So in Prague, routes met that led as far as Córdoba and Kyiv.[25]

What was sold there? Furs from the east. Saddles, bridles and shields, made right there in Prague. And above all, slaves. The slave trade was what interested Ibrahim most on his journey.[25]

Ibrahim was also struck by the local currency. Besides coins, people in Prague paid with small cloths. Ten of these cloths were worth one coin. It sounds like a fairy tale, but one such cloth has actually been found: in a 9th-century grave at Uherské Hradiště in Moravia.[25]

And it was under Boleslaus that the first Bohemian coins began to be struck in Prague: silver denarii bearing the duke's name. Numismatists argue about the exact year. Some date them as early as around 955, others only to the end of Boleslaus's reign. But the fixed rate between the cloths and the coin suggests that denarii were already in circulation in Ibrahim's time.[17][22][25]

So what did Ibrahim's Prague look like? The market lay below the castle, in what is now Malá Strana, the Lesser Town. Above it, according to the Arabic account, stood a great fortified stronghold.[18][25]

But the whole city was not built of stone and lime. Even at the castle, archaeologists have found wooden houses and paths laid with logs. The churches were of stone. And the rampart had a stone face, though otherwise it was built of timber and earth. Perhaps it was this rampart that impressed Ibrahim.[1][3][14][19]

A rich duke of a rich city now lacked only one thing: his own bishop.[11][12]

7. Prague gets a bishop

Until then, in church matters, Bohemia had come under the bishop of Regensburg in Bavaria. Wenceslas himself had to ask him for permission when he wanted to build a church. A duke who wanted to be master of his own land needed a bishop in Prague.[9][11]

Boleslaus the First did not live to see it. He died in 972. The negotiations were completed shortly afterwards by his son, Boleslaus the Second. The bishopric of Prague was established in 973.[11][22]

The bishop of Regensburg, of course, resisted, since he was losing a large part of his diocese. Disputes within the empire also played a part. So the first bishop of Prague was not consecrated until January 976.[11]

His name was Dětmar. He was a Saxon monk from the monastery of Corvey, and the duke chose him for a practical reason: he spoke Slavonic. He had originally come to Bohemia as a pilgrim. He was consecrated by the archbishop of Mainz.[11]

Dětmar died at the beginning of 982. At Levý Hradec, a Czech was elected as his successor. His name was Vojtěch, known in the West as Adalbert, and he came from the powerful Slavník family, whose seat was at Libice.[2][11][20][21]

Vojtěch had studied in Magdeburg for nine years. He was consecrated bishop in 983, in Verona in Italy.[20][21]

And in Prague, he ran into trouble. He wanted his flock to live as Christians. According to the legends, he objected to the nobles taking several wives, to married priests and to the sale of Christian slaves. In other words, to exactly the trade that was making Prague rich.[20][25]

Vojtěch gave up and left for Rome, where he entered a monastery. Years later, at the urging of people in Bohemia, he returned. He brought monks from the Roman monastery with him, and in 993, together with them and the duke, he founded the first monastery for men in Bohemia, at Břevnov, today part of Prague.[20][21]

But the reconciliation did not last. Vojtěch left a second time and never came back to Prague. He set out to preach Christianity to the pagan Prussians on the Baltic. There, in April 997, they killed him.[20][21]

Two years earlier, though, a blow had fallen that struck his whole family.[10][20]

8. Libice and the Slavníks

Libice lies where the Cidlina flows into the Elbe, east of Prague. In the second half of the 10th century, it was the seat of the Slavník family. Vojtěch was born here too, around 955.[5]

At their stronghold, the Slavníks built a church modelled on Saxon Ottonian architecture, the finest building of its day in the empire. Its ground plan is marked out on the site today.[5]

They even minted their own coins. These bear the name of Vojtěch's brother Soběslav, the head of the family. How great their power was is a matter of debate among historians. The older view made them lords of a large part of Bohemia. Jiří Sláma believes that in 995, their power barely reached beyond Libice.[12][17][25]

It came at the end of September 995. Soběslav was far away with his warriors, on King Otto the Third's campaign against the Polabian Slavs, along the Elbe. Bishop Vojtěch was living in a monastery in Rome. Their brothers and their families had stayed behind at Libice.[20][25]

On Friday, 27 September, the Přemyslid retinue laid siege to the stronghold. The besieged reportedly begged to be allowed to celebrate the next day's feast of St Wenceslas in peace. According to the legend, the answer was: “If your saint is Wenceslas, ours is Boleslaus.” The next day the stronghold fell. All the Slavníks, small children included, were slaughtered in front of the castle church.[10][25]

If your saint is Wenceslas, ours is Boleslaus.
Bruno's Legend of St Vojtěch (Adalbert), cited by J. Sláma; our translation

Four of Vojtěch's brothers were killed. In another passage, Cosmas writes of five and gives their names, but the older legend by Bruno speaks of four.[10][12]

Who gave the order? Bruno's legend blames Duke Boleslaus the Second directly: he is said to have broken a truce. Cosmas, who wrote much later, defends the duke and puts the blame on the nobles. The duke really was seriously ill at the time. Historians still disagree about it. What is certain is that the Přemyslid retinue profited from the fall of Libice.[10][12][25]

And now a surprise. Archaeologists expected to find a thick layer of burning at Libice. They did not. The year 995 is barely visible in the ground.[5]

The stronghold simply lived on. It served as an administrative centre until the middle of the 12th century. That does not disprove the massacre of the Slavníks. It only shows that the end of a family was not the end of the place. And also that archaeologists have so far excavated only about five percent of the hillfort.[5]

So at the end of the 10th century, a single dynasty rules Bohemia. It has a capital, its own coins and its own bishop.[5][11][12]

9. What the first Přemyslids left behind

Let's take five things away from today's episode.

First: Bořivoj is the first Přemyslid we know from historical records. He was baptised from Great Moravia. And when Moravia weakened, his sons turned to Bavaria in 895.[6][8][13]

Second: the first Přemyslids controlled only the centre of Bohemia. They relied on strongholds around Prague: Levý Hradec, Budeč, Tetín. You can stand on all three today.[2][15][16][25]

Third: Wenceslas was murdered on 28 September, most likely in 935. His brother Boleslaus then turned a small duchy into a realm reaching as far as Kraków.[7][22][25]

Fourth: tenth-century Prague was an international marketplace. Furs and people were traded here, and payment was made in small cloths and soon in the first Bohemian denarii. From 973, it had its own bishopric.[11][17][25]

Fifth: in 995, the Slavníks were massacred at Libice. The Přemyslids were left alone in Bohemia.[5][10]

Which of these places feels closest to you: Levý Hradec, Budeč, Tetín or Libice? Tell us which ones you have visited. And if you find a mistake in our account, write it in the comments, along with a source.

And what will a dynasty do once it has no rivals? We will see next time: Oldřich and Moravia, Břetislav, and the first Bohemian king. We continue with the Přemyslid state.
