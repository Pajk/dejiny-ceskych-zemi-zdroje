1. A Figurine from the Ashes

On 13 July 1925, archaeologists are working on a slope below the Pálava hills near Dolní Věstonice, in the south of Moravia, the eastern part of today's Czech Republic. The excavation is led by Karel Absolon of the Moravian Museum in Brno. In the ashes of a large prehistoric hearth, a broken-off piece of a leg made of fired clay appears first, and ten centimetres away the rest of the figure[14]. Put together, they make a figurine of a woman 111 millimetres long[13][14]. Today we know it as the Venus of Dolní Věstonice.

Above the left buttock the figurine bears the print of a child's finger[13]. Someone touched it while the clay was still soft. It is about 30,000 years old. We cannot be more precise. The figurine itself cannot be dated, and its age is estimated from finds around it. The nearest dated skeleton comes out at just under 30,000 to 31,000 years[14]. That is why you will find slightly lower figures elsewhere[13].

And yet the Venus stands almost at the end of the story we will go through today. The first people came to what is now Czechia hundreds of thousands of years earlier. We will look at the oldest finds from near Prague and from Brno, which are disputed to this day, at the Neanderthals of the Moravian caves, at the arrival of people of our own species, and at the mammoth hunters. We will finish at the end of the last Ice Age.

From all that time we have not a single written word. So we will always keep three things apart: what was found, how it is dated, and what is only interpretation.

Before we set out for the oldest finds, let us see how time is measured in prehistory at all.

2. Time without Dates

The Old Stone Age, the Palaeolithic, falls into the period geologists call the Pleistocene. The climate changed again and again. In the ice ages our landscape was an open loess steppe. In the warm periods between them, the temperature could rise 2 to 4 degrees above today's average, and forest covered most of the land[1].

At least twice the continental ice sheet from the north reached us too, long before the last Ice Age[5][21]. In Bohemia it touched the Šluknov and Frýdlant areas in the far north[21]. In northern Moravia and Silesia it covered the regions of Opava and Ostrava and pushed through the Moravian Gate as far as today's Nový Jičín and Kopřivnice. But it never crossed the watershed between the Oder and the Danube east of Hranice[5]. In the last Ice Age the ice stopped farther north, roughly in today's central Poland. The cold peaked twice. First about 65,000 years ago. Then about 22,000 years ago[2].

And how is such an age measured? For younger finds mainly by the radiocarbon method, which is based on the decay of radioactive carbon[1]. There is a catch. Radiocarbon years are not calendar years and have to be converted, or calibrated. For the mammoth hunters, today's calibration adds more than 4,000 years to the original figure[15]. Older overviews reckoned with three to four thousand[1]. That is why different books give different numbers for the same find.

For the oldest sites radiocarbon does not help at all. There the age is estimated from geological layers, from animal remains, especially small rodents, and from measuring the magnetism of rocks[3]. The result is not a year but a range.

With that in mind, let us look at the oldest traces of humans in our lands.

3. The Oldest Traces: Přezletice and Stránská skála

Přezletice lies just beyond the north-eastern edge of Prague. After the Second World War, František Prošek found the first stone tools here, and archaeological excavation then continued until 1990. It was led by Jan Fridrich of the Institute of Archaeology in Prague[3][1].

What was found? According to the excavators there was a small lake here, about three metres deep, with blocks of a hard rock called lydite rising out of it. They recovered 729 chipped stone tools, mostly made of that lydite, and broken animal bones with cut marks. Some of the bones were burnt. They belonged to thirty-seven species of mammals, among them an ancestor of the mammoth, a rhinoceros, a bison and a horse[3].

How old is it? There is no reliable direct date. One species of vole was found at Přezletice, but another, which appears in Central Europe only later, is missing. That points to an age of about 700,000 years. Measuring magnetism gave 590,000 to 660,000 years, but the excavators themselves call that result unsound, because the samples were not taken in a controlled way[3].

And the interpretation? Jan Fridrich described an oval bank of stones, clay and large bones measuring 4 by 3 metres, with a small hearth at the entrance, and interpreted it as the remains of a hut[3]. A more recent overview of the Palaeolithic is more cautious. It speaks only of "possible ground plans of dwellings" and points out that the methods for proving such structures at the oldest sites are still being developed[1].

Přezletice has one more catch. Excavation began here after a fragment of a tooth was found and first identified as a human molar. It later turned out to have belonged to a bear[27]. And some researchers abroad doubt the tools themselves. Lydite also cracks on its own, and a natural fragment is hard to tell from a worked one. Jan Fridrich admitted as much. In 1995 the Dutch archaeologists Wil Roebroeks and Thijs van Kolfschoten wrote that they saw no convincing traces of human work on the Přezletice stones[27]. Czech overviews of the Palaeolithic still count Přezletice in[1]. The dispute is not settled.

From a similarly distant time comes the site of Stránská skála in Brno. As early as the beginning of the 20th century, Jan Woldřich described a hearth with animal bones in a cave here. Experts argued about it for a long time. In 2006 the palaeontologist Rudolf Musil wrote that the latest research proves Woldřich right and that this is the oldest hearth in Central Europe[4]. But evidence of fire that old is disputed. A more recent review of European sites concluded that people in Europe began to use fire regularly only about 300,000 to 400,000 years ago[23].

And are these the oldest traces at all? That is not certain. From Červený kopec in Brno comes a battered pebble from layers perhaps 0.8 to 1.2 million years old. But it cannot be ruled out that nature shaped it[1].

Who were these people? They were not people of our species. European populations of that time are today usually called Homo heidelbergensis, although even that name is not settled[1].

Hundreds of thousands of years pass after the first traces before people appear in Moravia whom we also know from their bones: the Neanderthals.

4. Neanderthals in the Moravian Caves

The Neanderthals were the European, or more precisely the Eurasian, form of humans. In Moravia they left bones in three caves: Šipka, Švédův stůl and Kůlna. In every case these are only small fragments[1].

The first find came in 1880. In Šipka Cave near Štramberk, Karel Jaroslav Maška, a secondary-school teacher from Nový Jičín, found a piece of the lower jaw of a Neanderthal child[7][24]. The original no longer exists. In 1945 it burned in the fire of the chateau at Mikulov[7].

In 1905 a geology student, Karel Kubasek, recovered part of a human lower jaw in the cave Švédův stůl near Ochoz. A year later Anton Rzehak described it as Neanderthal[6].

But we know most from Kůlna Cave near Sloup in the Moravian Karst. It is a tunnel 92 metres long. From 1961 to 1976 it was excavated by Karel Valoch, who distinguished a series of layers one above another. In one of them, labelled 7a, stone tools of Neanderthals lay together with their bones[1].

How old are they? Here we reach the limit of the radiocarbon method. According to specialists, the dates from this layer are only minimum ages; the real age will be higher. An overview of the Moravian Palaeolithic places the Neanderthal occupation of the caves at roughly 90,000 to 45,000 years ago. Another method, measurements on teeth from the same layer, gave an age of about 50,000 years with an uncertainty of five thousand[22]. Exactly when the Neanderthals disappeared from Moravia we cannot date[1].

What do the finds say about them? At Kůlna almost 80 per cent of the stone used for tools comes from the near surroundings. So the Neanderthals moved within a rather limited territory. In the upper layers they hunted mammoth and reindeer most often[1].

Some time before 45,000 years ago, other people appear in Central Europe. This time they are people of our own species.

5. People of Our Species: Zlatý kůň and Mladeč

In 1950 an almost complete human skull was found in the Koněprusy Caves beneath the hill of Zlatý kůň, south-west of Prague[18]. It is kept in the National Museum in Prague[8]. Radiocarbon dates from it were inconsistent, some only around 15,000 years[18]. As late as 2009 an overview therefore placed the find at the very end of the Ice Age[1].

In 2021 came a turning point. An international team, with researchers from the National Museum and from the Faculty of Science in Prague, read the genome from the skull. In the bone they also found the DNA of a cow. The skull had once been consolidated with a glue made from cattle, and that made the radiocarbon dates too young[18].

So the scientists estimated the age in another way. People outside Africa carry a little Neanderthal DNA in their genome. The more time has passed since the interbreeding, the shorter its segments are. The woman from Zlatý kůň had longer ones than the 45,000-year-old individual from the Siberian site of Ust'-Ishim. She is therefore at least 45,000 years old. She lived about 70 to 80 generations after the last interbreeding with Neanderthals[18]. This is an estimate from genetics, not a direct date.

More recent work has shown that this woman was distantly related to people from a cave at Ranis in Germany, whose bones are roughly 45,000 years old. Their genomes are among the oldest genomes of our species read so far. But this group has no descendants among later Europeans[19][18].

From the same time, roughly 45,000 to 35,000 years ago, we know new ways of making stone tools from Moravia. One was named the Bohunician, after Bohunice in Brno[1]. Who made those tools is not certain. No bones that would prove it have been found with them[1].

We are on firmer ground in the Mladeč Caves near Olomouc. They were discovered during limestone quarrying in 1828, and more than a hundred human bones of several individuals were found in them, along with bone points and pierced animal teeth[10][1]. In 2005, human teeth from Mladeč were dated directly in Vienna. The result was roughly 31,000 radiocarbon years[20][9]. Converted to calendar years, that corresponds, by an older estimate, to about 35,000 years ago[1]. At the time it was the oldest directly dated larger group of modern human skeletons in Europe: men, women and juveniles[20].

The people of Mladeč belong to the culture called the Aurignacian. After it comes the period that made Moravia famous around the world: the time of the mammoth hunters.

6. The Mammoth Hunters

Archaeologists call this culture the Gravettian, and its older phase the Pavlovian, after Pavlov below the Pálava hills[1]. The densest settlement below the Pálava falls roughly between 31,000 and 29,500 years ago[2].

The settlements survived under a thick layer of loess. Besides Dolní Věstonice and Pavlov, the main ones are Předmostí near Přerov and Petřkovice in Ostrava[1]. The dwellings were usually about five metres across. Their appearance, a frame of wooden poles covered with hides, is derived by archaeologists from the buildings of peoples who still live in a similarly cold climate. It is a reconstruction, not a find[2].

But did they hunt mammoths at all? Mammoth bones lie in large dumps in damp gullies next to the settlements. Such dumps could have been created by people through long-term hunting. But they could equally have formed naturally, with people taking bones, tusks and meat from dead animals. On this basic question there is no clear answer[1]. Within the settlements themselves, by contrast, the bones of smaller animals prevail: hares, foxes, wolves, reindeer and birds[1]. Imprints of knots in lumps of fired clay from Pavlov show that people knew nets, which are well suited to hunting small game[1].

At Předmostí, one of the largest sites of its kind in Europe[11], Karel Jaroslav Maška, by then headmaster of a secondary school in Telč[24], made an extraordinary discovery in 1894: an accumulation of human skeletons. He himself wrote that "the remains discovered belong to at least 20 persons" – eight adults and twelve young ones, mostly children[1]. According to Maška, the grave was bounded by two mammoth shoulder blades[1]. With later finds, the minimum number of individuals rose to 25[1]. Maška believed they had all died in a single disaster and been buried together. A more recent analysis of his diaries leans towards the view that the dead were laid in the same place one after another[25]. The question is not decided.

the remains discovered belong to at least 20 persons
Karel J. Maška, 1894 (quoted after J. A. Svoboda, Čas lovců, 2009)

Most of the skeletons from Předmostí will never be measured again. In 1945 they burned in the chateau at Mikulov, and only a few bones survived[25]. What remains are descriptions, casts, photographs and drawings[1][2].

But below the Pálava the mammoth hunters also left something we would not expect in the Ice Age: fired clay and the imprints of textiles.

7. Clay, Fibres and Graves below the Pálava

Let us return to the Venus of Dolní Věstonice. Karel Absolon began systematic excavation at Dolní Věstonice in 1924, and the famous find came the very next summer[14]. He wrote of it that the day "is written in golden letters in the history of Věstonice"[2].

The 13th of July 1925 is written in golden letters in the history of Věstonice
Karel Absolon (quoted after J. A. Svoboda, Dolní Věstonice – Pavlov, 2017)

The figurine has four punctures on the top of the head, two slits instead of eyes, and the lower part of the legs is missing[14]. What is it made of? Absolon thought it was a mixture of clay with crushed burnt bones and mammoth ivory. Non-destructive micro-CT scanning, published in 2024, did not confirm that. There are no bones or ivory inside. The material is loess with a natural admixture of small stones and tiny fossils, and the figure was shaped from a single piece[14].

The Venus is not alone. A whole set of other figurines of animals and people in fired clay was found below the Pálava. They lay around the hearths in the huts, where they were fired. All it took was a temperature of 500 to 700 degrees, which an ordinary hearth reaches[2]. They are among the oldest known objects of fired clay in the world[1][13]. But the mammoth hunters did not make vessels from it. Ceramic pots come only many thousands of years later, with the farmers[2].

Fired clay also preserved what would otherwise have rotted: fingerprints of adults and children, animal hairs, and also crossing fibres, twisted cords and knots. The lumps are at most the size of a fingernail, so we do not know exactly what the textiles were used for[2].

And then there are the graves. In 1986, during a rescue excavation, archaeologists uncovered the grave of three young people[2]. Their skulls were covered with red pigment. The individual in the middle had a pathologically altered skeleton, perhaps from birth, and its sex could not be determined from the bones. Only DNA analysis in 2016 showed that all three were men. Two of them had the same maternal lineage, so they may have been brothers[15]. Why they died we do not know[15]. The radiocarbon date of the grave corresponds, after calibration, to roughly 31,000 years ago[15].

But the large settlements below the Pálava did not last for ever. The climate was cooling again.

8. The End of the Ice Age

About 27,000 to 26,000 years ago the hunters began to leave the slopes of the Pálava and moved towards the Danube, the Váh and farther east. A few thousand years later the ice sheet advanced into central Poland. Trees gave way to tundra, the herds left and the hunters followed them[2]. Over the abandoned camps the wind blew several metres of loess[2].

People returned in larger numbers only as the climate warmed. The culture we call the Magdalenian came, according to the prevailing interpretation, from the west, and most of its dates from our lands fall around 15,000 years ago[1]. In Moravia it settled mainly the caves of the Moravian Karst, in Bohemia the Bohemian Karst. That is where the large settlement of Hostim above the river Berounka lies[1].

The best known is Pekárna Cave near Mokrá. From it come two horse ribs with engravings. On the first, found by Karel Absolon and Rudolf Czižek, there are fighting bison. The second, with grazing horses, was discovered in front of the cave in 1963 by Bohuslav Klíma[16].

Meanwhile the climate kept changing. Then came two marked warm spells. The first about 15,000 years ago. The second some fifteen hundred years later. An open taiga of pine and birch spread across the landscape[1]. In the upper layers of Kůlna we no longer find reindeer but red deer, elk, wild boar and beaver[1].

Hunters and gatherers stayed here in the forest too. We call this time the Middle Stone Age, the Mesolithic. From rock shelters in northern Bohemia we have its dates in a range of roughly 12,000 to 7,500 years ago. It ends at the moment when the first farmers arrive in central Bohemia[1].

And it is right here, on the threshold of farming, that today's episode ends. What remains is to sum up what to take from it.

9. What to Remember

From the oldest period we have no undisputed human bones from our lands, only stone tools, and even those are argued over by some researchers[27]. At Přezletice near Prague they are about 700,000 years old, and that age is an estimate, not an exact date[3].

We know the Neanderthals from the Moravian caves Šipka, Švédův stůl and Kůlna[1]. People of our species lived here at least 45,000 years ago, as the genome of the woman from Zlatý kůň has shown[18].

About 30,000 years ago the mammoth hunters left behind settlements, graves, figurines of fired clay and imprints of textiles[1][2]. And with numbers from prehistory, the method and the conversion always matter[1].

You can see much of this for yourself. The finds from Dolní Věstonice are held by the Moravian Museum in Brno[13], the Archeopark Pavlov stands right on the site[2][12], and at Předmostí there is the Mammoth Hunters Memorial[11].

And what do we not know? Did people of our species meet the last Neanderthals in Moravia? And what happened to the people of Zlatý kůň, that they left no trace in later Europeans?[19][1] So far nobody has an answer to that.

Next time we will look at the people who began to change the landscape: the first farmers.
